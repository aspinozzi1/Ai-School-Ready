Here's the uncomfortable math. A 2025 RAND survey found that 53% of ELA, math, and science teachers and 54% of students said they used AI for schoolwork in 2024-25. The same report's headline finding: use is climbing quickly while guidance lags behind.

Translation for a building leader: AI adoption in your school is not a decision you get to make. It already happened. The only decision left is whether the adults are prepared for it.

You don't need a task force to find out. You need fifteen minutes and four questions. Grab a coffee and be honest.

## 1. Do your staff know the one hard rule? (4 minutes)

The rule: no student personally identifiable information goes into a public AI tool, ever. Not names, not IEP details, not discipline stories, not "a kid in my third period who just moved here from Ukraine."

Walk down the hall and ask three teachers what the school's AI privacy rule is. If you get three different answers, or three blank looks, that's your finding. This is the highest-stakes item on the list and the cheapest to fix: the rule plus the test that goes with it ("could anyone identify this child from what I typed?") can be taught to a full staff in ten minutes.

## 2. Is there an interim stance in writing? (4 minutes)

Notice I said stance, not policy. A policy takes a committee a year. A stance takes a page: here's what staff may do, here's the one hard rule, here's who to ask, here's when we'll revisit. As of April 2025, at least 28 states had published K-12 AI guidance, so you're likely not starting from zero; your state document is the skeleton.

Check: can you put your hands on a written AI stance for your building right now? If it lives in your head, or in a "we should really" conversation from last spring, it doesn't exist. Teachers without a written stance don't stop using AI. They just stop telling you about it, and that's the worst version of this.

## 3. Who is your natural AI lead? (3 minutes)

Every building already has one. The teacher whose sub plans got suspiciously good. The one colleagues already ask "how did you make that?" You're not looking for a technologist; you're looking for a respected peer who's a step ahead and generous about it.

Write down a name. If you can't, ask a department head; they'll know within seconds. This person is your future facilitator, your staff's "who do I ask" answer, and the difference between PD that lands and PD that's endured. Fancy title optional. A little release time and public backing: not optional.

## 4. Does your PD plan include hands-on time? (4 minutes)

Pull up whatever AI PD you've scheduled or sat through. Was there a block where every teacher had a device open and actually tried something, with help in the room? Decades of research summarized by the Learning Policy Institute in 2017 found that effective professional development requires active learning, collaboration, and sustained duration. A one-hour lecture about AI has none of those, which means it will change nothing.

If your plan is a speaker and a slide deck, mark this one failed and don't feel bad. Most plans fail here. The fix is structural, not expensive: protect a hands-on lab block, and schedule short follow-ups so the learning survives contact with October.

## Scoring

Four yeses: you're ahead of most of the country. Two or three: normal, fixable, start with question one because privacy risk is live today. Zero or one: still fixable, and better to know now than after an incident lands on your desk.
