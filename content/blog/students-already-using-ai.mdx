Whether your school has an AI policy or not, AI is in your school. A Pew Research Center survey of 1,458 teens, run in the fall of 2025, found that 54% of U.S. teens use AI to help with schoolwork, and 57% use it to search for information.

That's not a prediction. That's last year.

So the question isn't whether your staff will deal with AI. It's whether they'll deal with it having been taught anything at all. And here's the good news: the single most important lesson takes about ten minutes.

## The 10-minute rule

Here it is: sometime this week, at a staff meeting or a PLC or over email if you must, spend ten minutes teaching every adult in the building the One Hard Rule.

**Never put student personally identifiable information into a public AI tool.**

That's the whole rule. No student names. No initials attached to a class roster. No IEP details, no discipline write-ups, no health information, no family situations. A public chatbot is not a locked filing cabinet; the Future of Privacy Forum warned in 2024 that open-ended AI chat interfaces create real risk of student information disclosure, and that schools need to treat these tools under FERPA and state privacy law like any other vendor.

Teachers don't break this rule out of carelessness. They break it out of helpfulness. "Help me write an email to Marcus's mom about his behavior in third period" feels like a normal request. It's also a student's name, behavior record, and family contact context typed into a system the district doesn't control.

## The test that makes it stick

Rules fade. Tests stick. So pair the rule with a question staff can ask themselves every single time, before they hit enter:

**"Could anyone identify this child from what I just typed?"**

If the answer is yes, or even maybe, strip it down. "Help me write a firm but warm email to a parent about repeated disruptions in class" gets you the same draft with nobody's kid in it. The teacher adds the name and the specifics after the text leaves the AI tool and lands in her own email.

Notice what this test protects against that a simple "no names" rule misses. "A 7th grader at our school with a peanut allergy and a twin sister" has no name in it. It's also perfectly identifiable to anyone who knows the building. The test catches it; the rule alone doesn't.

## How to run the ten minutes

You don't need slides. Here's the agenda:

1. State the rule, out loud, in one sentence. (One minute.)
2. Show one unsafe prompt and its safe rewrite, like the parent email above. (Three minutes.)
3. Teach the could-anyone-identify-this-child test. (Two minutes.)
4. Let staff rewrite one unsafe prompt themselves, with a partner. (Three minutes.)
5. Tell them where to bring questions. Name an actual person. (One minute.)

That last step matters more than it looks. RAND reported in 2025 that AI use in schools is climbing fast while guidance lags well behind, which means your staff are making judgment calls alone right now. Giving them one rule, one test, and one person to ask beats a 30-page policy nobody reads.

## Why this comes first

There's plenty more to teach about AI: prompting, lesson planning, checking output for errors. All of it can wait a week. Privacy can't, because the risk exists on day one and the fix is cheap.

Ten minutes. One rule. One test. Do it this week, and every other AI conversation in your building gets safer.
