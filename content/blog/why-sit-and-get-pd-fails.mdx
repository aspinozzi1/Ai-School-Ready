You know the scene. It's a Tuesday after dismissal. The whole staff files into the cafeteria, someone dims the lights, and a presenter you've never met clicks through 74 slides. You grade papers under the table. By Friday, you couldn't name two things from the session.

Nobody in that room is lazy. The format is broken.

## What the research actually says

In 2017, Linda Darling-Hammond and her colleagues at the Learning Policy Institute reviewed decades of studies on teacher professional development and identified the features that show up again and again when PD actually changes practice. The list includes a focus on real teaching content, active learning, collaboration among colleagues, models of what good practice looks like, coaching and expert support, time for feedback and reflection, and sustained duration rather than a single event.

Read that list once more, and then picture the cafeteria slideshow.

No active learning: you sat. No collaboration: you were told not to talk. No coaching: the presenter left at 4:15 and you'll never see them again. No sustained duration: it was one afternoon, ever.

Sit-and-get PD doesn't fail because the topics are wrong. It fails because it's missing nearly every ingredient the research says matters.

## What self-run PD does differently

By self-run PD, I mean a session led by someone in your own building: a teacher, a coach, an AP, working from a prepared script and materials. That one change quietly fixes most of the list.

Active learning becomes easy to protect, because a colleague facilitating for peers isn't performing; she can say "okay, laptops open, try it" and walk the room. Collaboration is built in, because the people at your table are the people you actually plan with. The models of practice come from your own grade levels and your own kids.

And the coaching piece, the one districts usually pay the most for, is the one a building already has. The facilitator doesn't fly home. She's in the room next to yours in October when you get stuck, which is exactly when the research says support matters.

Sustained duration is the last piece. A single session, even a great one, fades. A session plus three ten-minute follow-ups at PLC meetings over the next month is a different thing entirely. That structure costs almost nothing. It just has to be planned in advance instead of hoped for.

## What you can do tomorrow

If you have any say in how PD runs in your building, three small moves get you most of the way there:

- Cut presentation time in half and give the recovered minutes to hands-on practice. Protect that practice block the way you'd protect instructional time. When the session runs long, cut discussion, never the lab.
- Name a follow-up date before the session ends. "We'll spend ten minutes on this at the September 12 PLC" turns an event into a sequence.
- Pick a facilitator from inside the building. Being a peer isn't a compromise. It's the delivery mechanism for the collaboration and coaching the research calls for.

None of this requires a budget line or an outside expert. It requires a decent script, a realistic clock, and a colleague willing to stand up front.

Teachers don't resist learning. They resist sitting still while someone reads slides at them. Build the session around doing, talking, and coming back to it, and the same staff that graded papers in the dark will surprise you.
