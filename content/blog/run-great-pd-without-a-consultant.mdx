There's a quiet assumption in how schools buy professional development: real training requires an outside expert. Somebody with a wireless mic and a hotel receipt.

The research doesn't support the assumption. When Linda Darling-Hammond and her Learning Policy Institute colleagues reviewed effective teacher PD in 2017, the features that mattered were things like active learning, collaboration among colleagues, coaching and support, feedback and reflection, and sustained duration over time. Notice what's not on the list: an outside presenter.

In fact, the most expensive features to buy from a vendor are the ones your building already owns.

## The coach you already have

Sustained support is where outside PD collapses. The consultant is excellent for ninety minutes, and then she's on a plane, and the first time a teacher hits a real problem in week three, there's no one to ask.

Now compare that to a session facilitated by a teacher in your own building. When someone gets stuck in October, the "expert" is across the hall with a plan period. That's coaching and sustained duration, the two features districts pay the most to approximate, delivered by proximity for free.

The trade-off is real, and let's name it: your in-house facilitator has never done this before, and she's nervous. That's the actual problem to solve. Not "who's the smartest person we can rent," but "what does a capable, nervous colleague need to run a great session on her first try?"

Three things.

## 1. A real script, not an outline

An outline ("10 min: discuss privacy") strands a first-timer the moment the room goes quiet. A script gives her the actual words: how to open, how to set up the activity, what to say when she brings the room back together. Word-for-word text she can lean on when nerves hit, and depart from the moment she finds her own footing.

Experienced presenters improvise. First-timers deserve a floor to stand on. Nobody in the audience minds; they mind aimlessness, not preparation.

## 2. A timing map with a printed escape hatch

The number one first-timer failure is the clock. The opening discussion runs hot, everything slides, and the hands-on block (the part the research says matters most) gets eaten.

So the script needs a running clock beside it: "by 3:20 you should be starting the practice activity." And it needs one standing order for when time gets tight: cut discussion, never the lab. Talk is the renewable resource; practice time is the point of the session. A pre-planned shorter cut of the session, on paper, beats improvised triage every time.

## 3. The hard questions, pre-answered

Every AI session gets the same handful of curveballs. "Is this even allowed?" "What about cheating?" "I heard it makes things up." "I don't have time to learn another tool."

A first-time facilitator who fumbles those loses the room. One who answers them calmly, in two sentences, wins it. So write the FAQ in advance: the honest answer to each, including "here's what we don't know yet," which is often the most credibility-building sentence in the whole session. Skeptics asking hard questions aren't a threat to the session. Answered well, they're the proof it's serious.

## What this costs

A script, a timing map, an FAQ page, and a colleague with the standing to lead: that's the whole bill of materials. Add the follow-up structure (a few ten-minute touchpoints at PLCs over the next month, scheduled before the session ends) and you've matched the research feature list more closely than most keynotes ever will.

The consultant model treats expertise as something you import. The research suggests it's something you develop, in-house, in the same people who'll sustain it after the session ends.
