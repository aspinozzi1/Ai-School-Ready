I still remember the week I realized AI could give me back my planning period. I wasn't trying to be cutting-edge — I was just tired of writing the same parent email for the fourth time on a Tuesday night. A colleague showed me a prompt template, I tweaked it for my own classroom, and I got twenty minutes back before dinner. That's really all this is: small, repeatable wins that add up over a semester.

Before I share them, one non-negotiable. I never, ever paste a student's name, grades, IEP details, or anything identifying into a public AI tool. Not because I got in trouble once (I didn't), but because it's just not worth the risk, and honestly, it's not necessary. Every prompt below works with placeholders like "a 7th grade student" or "[Student Name]" that you fill in yourself, offline, after the AI does the generic heavy lifting. Treat public AI tools the way you'd treat a substitute you've never met: helpful, capable, but not someone you hand a student's file to.

With that out of the way, here are the five prompts I use almost every week.

### 1. The Parent Email Draft

Parent emails eat more of my evening than anything else. This prompt gets me 90% of the way there — I just add the specific detail once I download the result.

```
Write a warm, professional email from a middle school teacher to a
parent/guardian. The purpose is to [briefly describe purpose, e.g.,
"share a positive update about class participation" or "request a
brief check-in about missing homework"]. Keep it under 150 words,
friendly but not overly casual, and end with an invitation to reply
or schedule a quick call. Do not include any names — use
[Student Name] and [Parent Name] as placeholders for me to fill in.
```

### 2. Differentiated Worksheet Questions

I teach three ability levels in every single class period, and rewriting one question three ways used to take forever. Now it takes thirty seconds.

```
I'm a [grade level] [subject] teacher. Here is a worksheet question:
"[paste the original question]"

Please rewrite this question into three versions:
1. A simplified version with more scaffolding for a student who
   needs extra support
2. The original version, lightly cleaned up for clarity
3. An extension version with added complexity for a student who
   needs an extra challenge

Keep the core learning objective the same across all three.
```

### 3. Rubric Generator

Rubrics are the thing I always mean to update and never have time for. This prompt gives me a solid draft I can adjust in five minutes instead of building one from scratch.

```
Create a 4-point rubric (Beginning, Developing, Proficient,
Exemplary) for a [grade level] assignment: [describe the assignment,
e.g., "a five-paragraph persuasive essay" or "a science lab report
on the water cycle"]. Include 3-4 criteria rows (such as content,
organization, evidence, and mechanics) with a short, specific
description for each performance level in each row. Format it as a
table.
```

### 4. Article Summarizer for Student Reading Levels

I teach a lot of current events and primary sources that are written well above my students' reading level. Instead of spending my planning period rewriting paragraphs, I let AI do the first pass and I edit for accuracy.

```
Rewrite the following article in student-friendly language for a
[grade level] reading level. Keep all key facts accurate, shorten
complex sentences, define any difficult vocabulary in parentheses
the first time it's used, and keep the total length to roughly
[300/500] words. Do not add opinions or information that isn't in
the original text.

Article text:
[paste article text here]
```

### 5. Discussion Questions and Exit Tickets

Nothing kills a good lesson faster than a weak closing activity thrown together in the last five minutes. This prompt gives me options I can pick from instead of starting cold.

```
I just taught a [grade level] lesson on [topic]. Generate:
- 3 open-ended discussion questions that require students to explain
  their reasoning, not just recall facts
- 2 exit ticket questions that can be answered in one or two
  sentences and would show me whether a student understood the
  main idea

Keep the language accessible for the grade level listed above, and
avoid yes/no questions.
```

### A Few Honest Notes on Using These Well

These prompts are starting points, not finished products. I always read what comes back before I use it — sometimes the tone is off, sometimes a "student-friendly" rewrite still uses a word my students don't know, and sometimes a rubric criterion doesn't quite match what I actually taught. AI is a fast first draft, not a substitute for your professional judgment. And again: keep it generic. If a prompt result needs a real name, a real grade, or a specific accommodation, add that by hand after you've reviewed the output — never before.
