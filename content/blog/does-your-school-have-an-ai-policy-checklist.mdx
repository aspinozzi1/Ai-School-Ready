If you asked five teachers what's allowed with AI right now, would you get five different answers? If so, you're not alone. Most schools are exactly where you are: teachers are already experimenting with AI tools, parents are starting to ask questions, and there's no single page that says "here's how we do this."

The good news is that a real AI policy doesn't need to be a 40-page document in a binder. It needs six or seven decisions, written down, that you can defend to a parent, a school board, or a state auditor. Grab a coffee — this will take about 15 minutes.

## Why This Can't Wait for a Committee

AI policy often stalls in committee because it feels like it needs to be comprehensive before it's official. It doesn't. A short, clear policy that covers the real risks — especially student privacy — beats a perfect one that never gets published. Staff are making AI decisions every day whether or not you've written anything down. The goal is to catch up quickly, not to solve every hypothetical.

## The One Rule That Matters Most

Before the checklist, one principle deserves its own paragraph: **never put student personal information into a public AI tool.** Names, grades, IEP details, behavior notes, health information, addresses — none of it belongs in a free chatbot's text box, because you have no control over where that data goes next. Everything else in your policy can evolve. This rule can't. If your staff internalizes nothing else, make it this.

## The 15-Minute Checklist

Work through this with your team, or solo as a first draft. Each box is a decision your school needs to have made — not perfected, just made.

- [ ] **We have a one-paragraph acceptable-use statement for AI.** A plain-language statement of what staff and students may and may not do, posted somewhere visible, beats any lengthy policy nobody reads.

- [ ] **We have a written rule against entering student PII into public AI tools.** This is the non-negotiable from above — put it in writing, not just a hallway conversation, so it's enforceable and teachable.

- [ ] **Staff have received at least one training on what's safe and what isn't.** Even a short session prevents the most common mistake: a well-meaning teacher pasting a class roster or an IEP into a chatbot to save time.

- [ ] **Parents have received a clear communication about how AI is used at school.** A short letter home builds trust before an incident forces the conversation — silence reads as "nothing is being managed."

- [ ] **We've named who actually owns AI decisions.** Someone — a principal, curriculum director, or technology coordinator — needs to be the decision-maker, or every question becomes a months-long committee referral.

- [ ] **We have a review cadence, not a one-time policy.** AI tools change monthly. Put a recurring date on the calendar, even quarterly, to revisit what's working.

- [ ] **We know which AI tools are actually being used in classrooms.** You can't govern what you haven't inventoried — a quick staff survey often surfaces more tools than administrators expect.

- [ ] **We have a plan for what happens if something goes wrong.** A brief, calm answer to "what do we do if student information ends up somewhere it shouldn't" beats deciding during a crisis.

## What "Good Enough" Looks Like

You don't need legal language. You need something a reasonable parent can read in two minutes and feel reassured by, and something a board member can point to and say "we have that covered." Mostly checked above? You're in a strong, defensible position — genuinely ahead of most schools nationally.
