I made this mistake myself. Last spring I was drafting a behavior support plan and pasted a chunk of a student's IEP straight into a public AI chatbot to summarize the accommodations. Name, grade, disability category, all of it. I got a clean summary back in ten seconds. It wasn't until I was walking to my car that it hit me: I'd just handed a stranger's server a child's confidential file because it was convenient.

That's the mistake. It's not exotic or complicated, and almost every teacher I know has done some version of it.

## The mistake: pasting real student information into public AI tools

When it's 9 p.m. and we just need a differentiated worksheet or a parent email drafted fast, we type what's actually in front of us. That usually means:

- A student's full name, attached to their reading level or behavior notes
- Grades, test scores, or specific IEP and 504 details
- Quotes from a student's own writing, which can be identifying on its own
- Health, family, or home-life details a student or parent shared in confidence

None of that is malicious, it's just habit. But once it's typed into a public AI tool, you no longer control where it goes or who sees it downstream. Most of these tools aren't built for FERPA, and "they probably won't misuse it" is not a policy, it's a hope.

> The rule I now use with every prompt: if I wouldn't want it read aloud at a school board meeting, it does not go into an AI tool.

## The easy swap: de-identify before you type

The fix doesn't require you to stop using AI. It requires one extra step: strip out anything that identifies a real kid. Swap names for "Student A" or "the student," describe the situation generically, and leave out anything a parent or admin could recognize.

Here's what that looks like in practice.

### Example 1: The parent email

**Before:** "Write a firm but kind email to Jamie Torres's mom about Jamie failing three quizzes in a row and not turning in homework since March."

**After:** "Write a firm but kind email to a parent about their child failing three recent quizzes and missing homework for several weeks. I'll add the student's name and specific dates myself before sending."

### Example 2: The IEP-informed lesson plan

**Before:** "Create a modified assignment for Marcus, who has an IEP for dyslexia and processing speed delays, reading two grades below level."

**After:** "Create a modified assignment for a student reading two grades below level who benefits from shorter text chunks and extended processing time."

### Example 3: The behavior note

**Before:** "Summarize this incident report: Destiny, age 9, hit another student during recess after being teased about her home situation."

**After:** "Summarize a playground incident involving physical conflict between two students, triggered by teasing, for a general behavior log entry."

Notice the pattern: the AI still gives you a genuinely useful draft in every "after" version. You just add the real name and specific details afterward, in your own gradebook or student information system, never inside the AI chat window itself.

## Make it a five-second habit

The swap becomes automatic fast. Before you paste anything into an AI tool, ask yourself:

- Would this identify a specific student to someone reading it later?
- Am I including a name, a family detail, or a diagnosis?
- Could I generalize this and still get what I need?

If the answer is yes to the first two, generalize it first. That's the whole system.
