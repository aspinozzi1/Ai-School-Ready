A 2025 Gallup survey of 2,232 public K-12 teachers found that teachers who use AI weekly save about 5.9 hours a week. That's roughly six weeks over a school year. I'm not promising you six weeks. I'm promising you that the first hour is easy, and it comes from exactly the tasks below.

One ground rule before the prompts: AI drafts, the teacher decides. Every one of these produces a draft. You read it, fix it, and make it yours before it ever reaches a student or a parent. Nothing goes out unreviewed, and no student names or details ever go in.

## 1. The newsletter blurb

```
Write a warm, brief classroom newsletter blurb for families of [grade level]
students. This week we worked on [topic]. Next week we start [topic].
Include one sentence on how families can help at home. Keep it under
120 words and skip the exclamation points.
```

Newsletters eat a surprising amount of Sunday. The draft won't sound like you yet; edit two sentences and it will. That's five minutes instead of twenty-five.

## 2. The leveled text

```
Rewrite the following passage at a [2nd/4th/6th] grade reading level.
Keep all the key facts. Keep the same order of ideas. Use shorter
sentences and simpler vocabulary, but do not make it babyish.
Passage: [paste text]
```

Differentiating a reading is the task teachers most often do by hand at 9 p.m. Check the output carefully for facts that got simplified into being wrong; it happens, and you're the one who'll catch it.

## 3. The quiz with answer key

```
Create a 10-question quiz on [topic] for [grade level]: 6 multiple
choice, 2 short answer, 2 true/false. Match these learning goals:
[paste goals]. Provide an answer key at the end with a one-line
explanation for each answer.
```

The answer key is where you do your quality control. Work the quiz yourself before you print it. If an answer is wrong or a question is ambiguous, fix it or cut it. You're grading the intern's work, not trusting it.

## 4. The rubric from your notes

```
Turn these rough notes into a 4-level rubric (Beginning, Developing,
Proficient, Advanced) for a [grade level] [assignment type]. Use
student-friendly language a [grade level] student could read.
My notes: [paste your messy notes]
```

You already know what good work looks like; you just haven't formatted it into a grid. This prompt turns ten minutes of formatting into one. The judgment stays yours because the notes were yours.

## 5. The warm-up variations

```
Here is a warm-up problem I like: [paste it]. Create 5 variations
at the same difficulty, then 3 slightly easier and 3 slightly
harder versions. Label each set.
```

One good warm-up becomes eleven, which covers a week and gives you ready-made options for students who need a different entry point. Solve a few of them yourself first, especially in math, where a "variation" can quietly change the difficulty or break the problem.

## The habit that makes this work

Notice the pattern in all five: you supply the judgment (the goals, the notes, the problem you already like) and the AI supplies the typing. That same Gallup survey found 64% of teachers who use AI say the materials they modify with it end up better quality. Modify is the key word. The teachers saving real time aren't pasting AI output into their classrooms; they're editing drafts instead of writing from blank pages.

Start with whichever prompt matches the task you dread most. Time yourself once by hand and once with the draft-then-edit routine. The hour finds itself.
