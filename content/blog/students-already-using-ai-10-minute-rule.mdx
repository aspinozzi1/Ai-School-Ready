Last fall, I watched a student pull up a chatbot on his phone during independent reading time and ask it to "make this sound smarter." He wasn't being sneaky — he genuinely thought this was just what you do now. And he's right, mostly. Our kids are already using AI, with or without our permission slips.

So the question isn't whether AI shows up in your classroom. It's whether you've got a habit that catches problems before they happen, instead of after.

That's where the 10-Minute Rule came from. It's not a policy binder or a training module you forget by Thursday. It's a check I run in my head — sometimes out loud, at my desk — before I introduce any AI tool to my students, and it takes about ten minutes.

## The 10-Minute Rule

Before any student-facing AI activity, I ask myself three questions. I once set a timer as an experiment — it took nine minutes. Close enough.

### Question 1: Does this put any student information into a public tool?

This is the one that matters most, so I'll say it plainly: **never put student personal information into a public AI tool.** Not names, not grades, not IEP details, not disciplinary notes, not "Jaylen, who sits in the back and struggles with fractions." None of it.

I know that sounds obvious. But it sneaks in. A teacher pastes an email thread into a chatbot to "help me respond professionally," and the email has a parent's name and the kid's attendance history in it. Another uploads a spreadsheet of reading levels, not realizing the file has full names and IDs. Neither meant harm. They just didn't pause for the ten minutes.

My rule of thumb: if I wouldn't want it printed on a flyer taped to the front office door, it doesn't go into a public AI tool. Ever.

> "I don't trust my judgment when I'm tired and it's 9 PM. The rule doesn't ask me to be careful. It asks me to check three boxes."

### Question 2: Is a human verifying the output?

AI is a confident liar. It will generate a reading comprehension question about a book chapter that doesn't exist, with the exact same tone it uses when it's right. So before anything generated by AI reaches a student's eyes, I ask who is checking it, and whether they actually checked it or just skimmed it.

In practice:

- I read every AI-generated worksheet or quiz question before it goes out — not after a parent emails me.
- If a colleague generated something, I ask what they checked for.
- Anything with facts, dates, or citations gets verified against a real source.

### Question 3: Is there a non-AI fallback?

The internet at my school has gone down twice this year, both times during a lesson built around a tool. If your activity only works when the AI works, you don't have a lesson plan — you have a hope. Before I commit to an AI-based activity, I make sure I could run the same class period without it.

This has saved me more than once, and it makes the AI parts better too, because I'm not leaning on the tool to do work I haven't thought through myself.

## Why ten minutes, not ten seconds

"Just be careful" isn't a habit — it's a hope, same as a lesson plan with no fallback. Ten minutes is short enough that I'll actually do it before first period, and long enough to catch the real mistakes: the pasted email with a name in it, the fact I didn't double-check, the activity that dies the second the wifi does.

## Make it a staffroom habit, not just yours

The rule works best when it's not just your private ritual:

- Print the three questions and tape them inside the supply closet where the good markers live. People actually go in there.
- Say it out loud in a PLC meeting before anyone shares a new AI activity: "Quick check first — PII, verification, fallback."
- Make it the first slide of any AI-related PD, every single time. Repetitive is the point.

None of this requires trusting a new system with your students' data. The rule works *because* no student information ever needs to leave your head and enter a tool in the first place.
