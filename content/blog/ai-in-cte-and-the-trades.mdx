Almost everything written about AI in education assumes you teach in a room with desks. I teach in a room with a table saw. The AI-in-schools conversation has mostly skipped CTE, which fits a pattern: a 2025 RAND survey found AI adoption among teachers is uneven across subjects. So let me offer the view from the shop floor: what I actually use, and what I'd never trust it with.

The short version: AI is useful for the paperwork that surrounds shop teaching, and useless for the teaching itself.

## Where it earns its keep

**Toolbox talks and safety briefings.** Every shop teacher gives short safety talks constantly, and writing fresh ones is a grind. I'll ask AI to draft a five-minute toolbox talk on kickback, or on eye protection, or on housekeeping around cords and offcuts. The draft is generic every single time. But generic is a fine starting point, because I know what's missing: the specifics of my shop, my machines, my kids' habits. I rewrite a third of it and I've still saved twenty minutes.

The rule I hold to: AI drafts, the teacher decides. Nothing safety-related goes in front of students until I've read every line against what's actually bolted to my floor.

**Leveled instructions for stations.** Shop classes are radically mixed-ability. At any station, the miter saw station say, I've got a student who could run it half-asleep and a student reading the instructions for the third time. AI is genuinely good at taking my station procedure and rewriting it simpler: shorter sentences, one action per step, key warning repeated. Same procedure, three reading levels, ten minutes. That used to be an evening.

**Safety checklists and inspection sheets.** Pre-operation checklists, end-of-class shutdown lists, PPE reminders formatted as signage. AI formats these fast from my rough notes. The content is mine; the typing is the intern's.

One line I never cross: no student information goes into a public AI tool. Not names, not accommodations, not "the kid who had the close call last week." Injury reports and anything with a student attached get written by me, on district systems, period.

## What AI can't do in a shop

This is the part the ed-tech world doesn't say plainly, so I will.

AI cannot read material. It has never held a board. It doesn't know what a knot near a rip cut means, or that this particular piece of stock is cupped just enough to matter. That judgment lives in hands and eyes, and my whole job is transferring it to students. There's no prompt for it.

AI cannot catch an unsafe setup. I can glance across the shop and see a fence that isn't locked, a guard that's been swung away, a stance that puts a hand where it shouldn't be. That's pattern recognition built from years of watching, and it happens in half a second at thirty feet. The federal Education Department's 2023 guidance on AI called for keeping humans in the loop, with educators able to inspect and override what AI suggests. In a shop, that's not a policy nicety. The human in the loop is the safety system.

And AI cannot judge readiness. Deciding a student has earned time on a machine is a call I make from watching that kid's habits for weeks. I wouldn't delegate it to another adult, let alone software.

## The honest summary for CTE teachers

Use AI for the words: talks, checklists, leveled instructions, the endless documentation. Keep it a hundred feet from the judgment: material, setups, readiness, anything where being wrong draws blood. That line is easy to hold once you name it out loud.

Trades teachers already know how to work with a capable but green apprentice. That's all this is. Hand it the paperwork; keep it off the machines.
