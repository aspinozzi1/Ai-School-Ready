How to use this script

Everything in plain type is meant to be spoken aloud. Read it verbatim (it's written for the ear) or glance and paraphrase once you're comfortable. Either way delivers a complete session. This is the Track A capstone: it assumes Kits 1 through 7 have run, and it spends their accumulated wins. If a kit was skipped, run it first; the capstone can't celebrate what didn't happen.

Meet Ms. Rivera. Throughout the deck you'll see the same example teacher's screen: Ms. Rivera, our running composite (not a real person; she appears in every kit). When her screen is up, point the room at it; it's the model to mimic in the lab. If someone asks who she is, say exactly that: a fictional teacher we use across the series so you always see what the work looks like before you try it yourself. Her tracker chip sits top-right on every slide; the matching line under each slide cue below tells you what it says, so you always know what the room is reading.

From the founders

This note is from Adam and Katelyn Spinozzi, the two certified teachers who wrote this kit. It's here to give you their perspective before you present. Sharing it is entirely your call: read parts aloud where they fit, retell them in your own words, or keep it to yourself as background. The session is complete either way.

We're Adam and Katelyn, and here is why this session matters for what happens in your classrooms: everything the last seven kits built is still fragile. Habits that live in individual teachers leave when teachers leave, fade when Octobers get hard, and never reach the hire who starts in January. The research on professional learning is clear about what makes it stick: sustained duration, collaboration, and time to practice together. Culture is the difference between a school that did some AI training once and a school where safe, skilled, human-centered practice is simply how the building works.

The lessons we believe this hour will leave with your staff: that norms written down and posted beat rules remembered; that a five-minute sharing routine keeps eight weeks of skills alive better than any refresher course; that skeptics, respected and recruited, are a culture's quality control; and that the certificate your staff earn today marks a beginning, not a finish line. The tools will keep changing. A culture that knows its rules, shares its wins, and protects its human core will be ready for every version of this technology that arrives after we're all done predicting it.

Now the honest threat. Across thirty-five rigorous studies, the professional learning that actually sticks shares the same features: sustained duration, collaboration, active learning, time to reflect. Thirty-four states and Puerto Rico have state-level guidance; your building is a different question. You are one faculty meeting away from being the exception, and this is that meeting.

Segment 2 · What culture means (slides 5–8)

SLIDE 5The culture test0:06

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her culture test: would a new hire learn the rules by watching, not by asking?

Here's the testable definition of culture: what a new hire learns by watching, before anyone tells them anything. If a colleague who joined tomorrow would see prompts shared openly, drafts reviewed before they ship, lane labels on assignments, and nobody pasting student names into a chatbot, you have a culture. If they'd have to be told, you have a policy. Policies inform; cultures train. Everything today aims at the watching version.

SLIDE 6The five norms0:08

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her five norms, drafted: privacy, review, honesty, sharing, the protected list.

A culture needs its rules short enough to live on one page. Five norms, each one earned by a kit you've already run. Privacy: no student information in public tools, ever. Review: nothing AI-drafted reaches a student or family unread. Honesty: we say what we use, to students and to families, and lanes are labeled. Sharing: working prompts go in the library; nobody solves twice. And the protected list: the relational work stays human, by decision. Your faculty will draft its own wording in the lab, but these five jobs need doing in any wording. One page, posted, signed. That's the constitution.

SLIDE 7The sharing routine0:10

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her sharing routine: five minutes, every PLC: one win, one fail, one steal.

The second load-bearing piece is a routine, because norms without a rhythm are posters. The proven shape is five minutes inside a meeting that already exists: one win, one failure, one steal. A win: something AI-drafted that worked. A failure, and this is the important one: something it got wrong, shared without embarrassment, because a culture that laughs about hallucinated citations is a culture that catches them. And a steal: one template someone's taking from the library this week. Five minutes. The whole apparatus of eight kits, kept alive by five minutes.

SLIDE 8The banner0:11

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her banner: a tool becomes culture when nobody has to remember to use it.

The banner over today: a tool becomes culture when nobody has to remember to use it. Calendars remember. Routines remember. Posted norms remember. People, in October, do not. Everything in the lab is about moving the remembering out of heads and into systems.

Segment 3 · The artifacts of culture (slides 9–12)

SLIDE 9The staff doc, grown up0:12

SLIDE 10Norms on one page0:15

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her norms, posted: five lines on one page, signed by the faculty.

The norms document, and the format matters: one page, five lines, plain words, signed. Not a binder; binders are where norms go to be forgotten. Ms. Rivera's faculty wrote theirs in the wording that sounds like their building and posted it in the workroom, and every teacher signed it, the way you sign something you mean. When leadership later builds formal policy, this page is its seed, and Kit 15 turns it into policy properly. Until then, this IS the school's stance, and it's a real one.

SLIDE 11The calendar0:17

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her calendar: monthly refreshers, Kit 1 for every new hire, norms revisited each semester.

Third artifact: the calendar. Culture needs dates, or it needs willpower, and dates are cheaper. The rhythm that works: the five-minute sharing round in every PLC or every other one. One kit refresher a month next semester, ten minutes each, using the 30-day plans you already own. Kit 1 for every new hire in their first month, run by any confident colleague. And the norms page revisited once a semester, because a stance that can't be revised becomes a relic. None of this is new meeting time; it's minutes inside meetings that already exist.

SLIDE 12Practice: culture triage0:19

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her triage eye: healthy culture shares failures; drifting culture hides use.

Interactive. Read each scenario, let the room call healthy or drifting, confirm with the answer strip at the bottom of the slide. 45-min cut: scenarios 1 and 3.

Quick practice: healthy or drifting? One: a teacher tells the whole PLC about the quiz question the AI got confidently wrong, and everyone laughs and checks their own. (Healthy; failure shared is quality control.) Two: a teacher quietly stops mentioning they use AI because a colleague rolled their eyes. (Drifting; use going underground is the first warning light.) Three: a new hire asks which chatbot the school uses and gets three confident, identical answers about the rules. (Healthy; that's the watching test passed.) Four: the staff doc hasn't gained an entry since September. (Drifting; the early-warning light. The fix is one sharing round, not a lecture.)

Segment 4 · Sustaining it (slides 13–17)

SLIDE 13Every new hire, covered0:21

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her onboarding plan: every new hire runs Kit 1 in their first month.

The culture's biggest leak is the January hire who never saw week one. The fix is already on your shelf: Kit 1 is self-contained and any confident colleague can run it for one person or five. The norm: every new hire experiences Kit 1 in their first month, gets the norms page in their welcome folder, and gets a library tour from a colleague. Thirty minutes of onboarding, and the culture stops depending on who was here in the fall.

SLIDE 14Measuring without surveillance0:23

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her measures: artifacts, not surveillance. Library growth, stories, exit tickets.

How do you know it's working without turning into the AI police? You count artifacts, never people. The library's growth. The exit tickets you already collect. The stories that surface in sharing rounds. Lane labels visible on assignments. What you never do is audit individual teachers' AI use, because surveillance kills the openness that makes the culture safe, and Kit 5 taught the whole building what happens when policing replaces clarity. Leadership: healthy cultures measure the garden, not the gardeners.

SLIDE 15When the tools change0:26

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her tool-churn insurance: habits that survive any vendor: rules, formula, review.

One reassurance, because somebody is wondering: every tool named in any headline will change. Interfaces, vendors, capabilities, all of it. Notice what this series never depended on: a specific product. The rules, the formula, the review habit, the lanes, the protected list: all of it transfers to whatever arrives next. That's not an accident; it's the design. Cultures outlive tools. That's the whole reason to build one.

SLIDE 16The never list0:27

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her never list: no policy lag, no mocked skeptics, no finish line.

The never list, capstone edition. Never let the written stance lag what staff actually do; revisit it each semester so paper and practice match. Never mock the skeptics: they are your quality control, your drift detectors, and the reason the review rule stays honest; recruit them, name their value out loud. And never treat today as a finish line: the certificate marks eight kits lived, not a subject completed. The schools that get this right stay students of it.
