AI & Academic Integrity: Ask for One Thing AI Can't Hand In

A 45–60 minute session in which every teacher rebuilds one real upcoming assignment so a chatbot cannot complete it. Run by your own coach; no consultant required.

The stance, and your parent answer
Assignments ask for something only this classroom could produce, every assignment says where AI stands in one line, and no student is ever accused based on an AI-detector score alone.

Why this matters now

54% of teens use AI for schoolwork (Pew, 2026) while guidance lags adoption (RAND, 2025). Detection does not close that gap: OpenAI withdrew its own classifier citing "its low rate of accuracy" (26% of AI text caught, 9% of human writing falsely flagged), and seven commercial detectors falsely flagged real TOEFL essays by non-native English writers at an average of 61.3% while classifying US eighth-grade essays near-perfectly (Liang et al., 2023). That is an equity and liability exposure, not a solution. Meanwhile self-reported cheating stayed relatively stable at roughly 60–70% before and after ChatGPT across the three high schools Stanford researchers surveyed (Lee, Pope et al., 2024). Three schools and self-report, not national causal proof, but enough to treat this as a design problem.

What your staff walks out with

  • One real assignment, rebuilt and ready to hand out this week
  • One sentence in it a chatbot is structurally unable to produce
  • A curiosity-first conversation for suspected misuse, built on process evidence
  • The red line, stated cold: no accusation on a detector score alone, ever
