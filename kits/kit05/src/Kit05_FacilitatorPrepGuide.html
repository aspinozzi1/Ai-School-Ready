AI & Academic Integrity: Ask for One Thing AI Can't Hand In

Everything you need before you present. Read this once (about 8 minutes), run the 15-minute prep checklist, and you're ready.

The whole session, in one sentence
Ask for one thing AI can't hand in.

What this session is

The session every faculty asks for, built around one move and nothing else. Staff see that they cannot reliably tell AI writing from student writing, that the software cannot either, and that the cheating rate did not spike when ChatGPT arrived. Then the move: add one thing to an assignment that a chatbot is structurally unable to produce, from one of three places. The 15-minute protected lab has every teacher rebuild a real assignment they are about to give.

If you remember one thing about facilitating this: resist the urge to add a system. Teachers will ask for a rubric, a matrix, a policy template. The answer is friendly and firm: one sentence, three places to look, and the assignment in front of you. Load is what broke the last version of this session.

Why the stakes are worth it

54% of teens use AI for schoolwork (Pew, 2026) while guidance lags adoption (RAND, 2025), and the detection route is worse than most staff realize: OpenAI withdrew its own classifier citing "its low rate of accuracy" (26% caught, 9% of human writing falsely flagged), and seven commercial detectors falsely flagged real TOEFL essays by non-native English writers at an average of 61.3% while classifying US eighth-grade essays near-perfectly (Liang et al., 2023). Stanford surveys at three high schools found self-reported cheating relatively stable at roughly 60–70% across ChatGPT's arrival (Lee, Pope et al., 2024). Assignment design is the problem to solve, not panic.

Who should present it

Same bar as Kits 1–4: any confident colleague. What you must hold firmly is the detector line. If someone advocates buying detection software, the answer is the evidence on slides 5 and 6, delivered kindly, then the red line on slide 25.

One prep item matters most: the pre-session email. The lab only works if people arrive with a real assignment they'll give in the next two weeks. Send the one-liner in the checklist below at least two days ahead. Second item: if your school has an existing academic-integrity or AI policy, skim it first. Today's move slots inside any policy, and where they conflict, your policy wins until leadership updates it.

Core session is 57 minutes. For a 45-minute slot: two practice prompts instead of three, five minutes on lab step 3 instead of six, two share-out voices instead of four. The script marks every cut. The lab never drops below fifteen minutes.

Your 15-minute prep checklist

  • Send the staff email (2 min, two days ahead): "Bring one real assignment you'll give in the next two weeks, in any subject. You'll leave with it rebuilt and ready to hand out."
  • Skim your school's existing integrity or AI policy (3 min): today slots inside it; flag any conflicts for leadership rather than resolving them live.
  • Read slides 5, 6 and 25 aloud once (5 min): the two detector slides and the red line are the passages you must deliver without hedging.
  • Run the four-second test yourself (3 min): paste an old assignment of yours into any chat tool. You will present slide 13 far better having done it.
  • Click through the deck (1 min): confirm all 30 slides display.
  • Print the handouts (1 min): Participant Handout, First 48 Hours, Exit Ticket, one of each per person.

Room, tech & timing

Projector, pair seating, a visible clock. Wi-Fi is optional; the lab is thinking and writing. The lab must start by 0:27, and the room must have done something out loud before the halfway mark (the vote at 0:01 and the practice at 0:22 both count). Cut discussion, never the lab. Expect real feelings; curiosity over accusation is for the adults too.

Facilitator FAQ

"Shouldn't we just buy a detector and be done?"

The evidence says no, and slides 5 and 6 give you the numbers. A tool that misses most AI text and misfires toward multilingual learners is not a policy.

"Isn't this just the AI Assessment Scale with fewer levels?"

Answer it straight; the credit is printed on slide 12. Naming a level of allowed AI use is prior art: the AI Assessment Scale (Perkins, Furze, Roe & MacVaugh, 2024) is the published five-level version, "No AI" through "AI Exploration." We teach one line because this session is about the assignment. Schools wanting a full scale should adopt the AIAS directly.

"Doesn't this make every assignment harder to grade?"

Slightly, and say so. Work anchored to your room has to be read by someone who was in the room, so it does not hand off to a substitute cleanly. In exchange you stop reading for forensics. Nobody is asked to do this to every assignment.

"What about our district's existing policy?"

It wins, always. One added sentence is classroom-level design, not policy replacement. Note any conflict and hand it to leadership; Kit 15 is where policy itself gets built. And if someone says their subject cannot be anchored to the room, the answer is place three: two minutes of the thinking, live, in front of them.

After the session: 5 minutes

  • Collect exit tickets; they double as PD documentation. Sort the added sentences by place: a department that used only one is a follow-up conversation, not a problem.
  • Send two strong sentences from different subjects to the whole staff.
