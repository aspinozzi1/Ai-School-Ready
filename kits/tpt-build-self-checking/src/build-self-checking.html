Before you start

What you need — all free

1. An AI chat

This pack is written with Claude on screen, because it is reliably good at producing a single working file and showing it to you straight away. ChatGPT and Gemini both work too — the prompts are plain English and are not tied to one product. Where a step depends on the specific tool, it says so.

Notepad on Windows, TextEdit on a Mac (switch it to plain text under Format), or the free editor of your choice. You are only ever saving text and double-clicking it. Nothing gets installed.

Chrome, Edge, Safari, Firefox — any of them. This is where your activity runs. Not a website, not an account: a file on your computer that the browser knows how to open.

A coding class. A paid subscription. Permission from anyone, for the version built in this pack. A web host. A domain name. Any prior experience.

Time: about 40 minutes the first time, most of it reading. Ten minutes the second time. Under five once it is a habit — that is the real prize here.

Read this before you build anything

The three-tier rule for student data

This page is first because it shapes what you build, not because it is paperwork. The activity in this pack sits entirely in Tier 1. Knowing the tiers is what lets you build the next thing safely.

1

Content you wrote yourself

Questions, word lists, problems, examples, explanations. No student appears in it at all. Build freely. Nobody needs to approve this. Everything in this pack is Tier 1.

2

De-identified student data

Scores and patterns with every identifier removed — "S1, S2, S3", or "a 3rd grader scored 6 of 10, missed every word with a blend." Useful, and safe if the identifiers never go in. Keep the roster key on paper or in your district's own system. The tool only ever sees S1.

3

Data that can be traced to a child

Names, class rosters, grades tied to a person, IEP details, anything a stranger could match to one student. Stop and ask your district. This is not a thing to stand up on a personal account, however convenient it would be.

The moment it goes wrong is almost always the same one.
You have a working Tier 2 tool. It shows S1, S2, S3. And you think: "I just need the names so I can tell who is who." That single step moves you from Tier 2 to Tier 3. Keep the key somewhere else and the tool stays safe.

This is practical guidance, not legal advice. FERPA, COPPA, your state law and your district's own policy are the actual authorities. Check your district's policy before any tool touches real student information.

How this actually works

The loop

Building with an AI chat is not one magic instruction. It is a short loop you run four or five times. Once you see the loop, every other build in the series is the same.

1 · Describe

Say what you want in plain sentences. Be specific about what the student does, not about how it should be coded.

2 · Look

Open what it made. Click it like a student would — including clicking the wrong answer on purpose.

3 · Say what is off

In one sentence. "When I click a wrong answer it does nothing." You do not need to know why.

4 · Repeat

Three or four rounds gets you something genuinely usable. It is a conversation, not a command.

The one habit that separates people who get this working from people who give up: describe the behavior you saw, not the fix you imagine. "It says correct even when I pick the wrong one" gets you further than "change the if statement", especially when you do not know what an if statement is.

A fair warning

The first thing it gives you is rarely the thing you want. That is normal and it is not you failing. Every build in this series assumes three or four rounds. Budget for them and the whole process stops being frustrating.

Step 1 of 6

Ask for the first version

Open your AI chat and paste this. It is long on purpose — every sentence in it exists because leaving it out produces something worse.

Prompt 1 · the first build
Build me a self-checking practice activity as one single HTML file I can save and open in a browser.

How it should work for a student:
• They see a list of questions, each with four answer choices as clickable buttons.
• If they click a wrong answer, that choice is grayed out and disabled, a message tells them to try again, and they can still pick another answer.
• If they click the right answer, it turns green, the other choices lock, and a sentence explains why that answer is correct.
• A counter at the top shows how many they got right on the first try — a wrong guess first should not count as a first-try correct.
• When every question is answered, show a summary and a button to start over.

Requirements:
• Everything in ONE file. No links to outside files, no internet needed, no login.
• Put my questions in a clearly marked list at the very top of the file so I can edit them without touching anything else.
• Add comments above that list telling me how to add or remove a question.
• Nothing is saved anywhere and no student name is ever asked for.
• Big text and big buttons — this may be used on a tablet.

Use five questions about rounding to the nearest ten as the example content so I can see it working.
Why "one single HTML file" matters. It is the difference between a thing you own and a thing you rent. One file goes on a shared drive, into your LMS, onto a flash drive, or into an email. Nothing expires and nothing needs an account.

Step 2 of 6

Four checks before you go further

Run these in order. If any fails, the sentence beside it is what you paste back.

Do thisIf it misbehaves, say this
Click a wrong answer on purpose. It should gray out and invite another try. "When I click a wrong answer, nothing happens. It should gray that choice out and let me try another one."
Then click the right answer. It should turn green and explain why. "When I get it right, it does not tell me why. Show the explanation under the question."
Check the counter. A question you got wrong first should NOT count as first-try correct. "The first-try counter is going up even when I guessed wrong first. It should only count questions I got right on my first click."
Answer everything. A summary should appear at the end. "Nothing happened when I finished the last question. Show a summary with how many I got right on the first try."
The third check is the one that catches people out. It is the most common thing to come back subtly wrong, and it is the thing that makes the activity honest: a student who guesses three times and lands on the right answer has not shown you they know it.

Step 3, continued

The part you edit

This is what the question list looks like in the finished file. Four things to notice.

{ q: "Round 47 to the nearest ten.", choices: ["40", "50", "47", "500"], answer: 1, why: "47 is past the halfway point (45), so it rounds up to 50." },
PartWhat it is
qThe question the student reads. Change the words, keep the quote marks.
choicesThe buttons, in the order they appear. Four is a habit, not a rule — three or five work.
answerCounts from zero. The first choice is 0, the second is 1, the third is 2. Here the answer is "50", which sits second, so it is 1.
whyWhat the student sees after answering. This is where the teaching lives.
Counting from zero is the one thing that trips everybody. If a question marks the wrong choice as correct, this is almost always why. Count the choices starting at zero and check the number matches.

Always test your own key

Click through every question once after you swap the content in. An activity that confidently marks the wrong answer correct is worse than no activity, and it takes ninety seconds to rule out.

Step 4 of 6

Make the feedback teach

A right-or-wrong activity is worth something. A right-or-wrong activity that explains itself is worth ten times as much, and it costs one more prompt.

Prompt 3 · better wrong-answer feedback
For each question, give each wrong choice its own short message explaining the likely mistake — not just "try again". Keep it to one sentence, keep it kind, and never say the student is wrong as a person. Show that message when they click that specific choice.

What that turns into

A student who clicks "40" on "round 47" sees:

"Close — 47 is past the halfway mark, so it goes up rather than down."

Rather than: "Not quite — try again."

This is the part where being a teacher beats being a programmer. You know which wrong answer means "guessed", which means "rounded the wrong way", and which means "does not know what halfway is." Those are three different students and they need three different sentences. Write them yourself.

Worth knowing

The included file uses one shared try-again message, to keep the code simple enough to read. Prompt 3 is how you upgrade it. Both versions are legitimate — start where you like.

Step 5 of 6

Make it look like your room

Prompt 4 · appearance
Change the look: use [your colors] as the main colors, make the question text larger, and put the activity title and a short instruction line at the top. Keep the buttons big enough to tap on a tablet.
Prompt 5 · accessibility, and this one matters
Make sure this works for a student using a keyboard instead of a mouse: every choice should be reachable by pressing Tab and selectable with Enter, with a clearly visible outline on whatever is currently selected. Do not use color alone to show right and wrong — include words too.
Prompt 5 is not optional and it is not decoration. A student using a keyboard, a screen reader, or with any color vision difference will hit a wall on an activity that signals "correct" with green alone. Asking for it costs one prompt. Retrofitting it later costs an afternoon.

How to check it worked

Put the mouse down. Press Tab repeatedly — you should see a clear outline moving between the choices. Press Enter on one — it should select. If either fails, say exactly that and ask again.

Step 6 of 6

Get it out of the chat and into a file

Until you do this, the thing you made lives inside a conversation. This step is what makes it yours.

1. Copy all of the code

Most tools have a copy button on the code block. Use it — selecting by hand usually misses the last line.

2. Paste it into a plain text editor

Notepad, or TextEdit set to plain text. Not Word. Word adds invisible formatting that stops the file working, and the failure is baffling when it happens.

3. Save it with .html on the end

Name it something you will recognize: rounding-practice.html. The .html is what tells your computer to open it in a browser. On Windows, set "Save as type" to All Files so it does not quietly become a .txt.

4. Double-click it

It opens in your browser. No internet needed. That is the whole thing — it works the same on a school laptop with the wifi off.

If it opens as a page of code instead of an activity, the file extension did not save. Rename it so it ends in .html and try again. This is the single most common stumble in this whole pack and it is a thirty-second fix.

When it breaks

The six things that actually go wrong

In order of how often they happen. Each fix is one sentence you paste back into the chat, or one thing you change yourself.

1. It opens as a wall of code

Cause: the file did not save with .html on the end. Fix: rename it so it ends in .html. On Windows you may need to turn on "file name extensions" in File Explorer's View menu to see what it really saved as.

2. Nothing happens when you click

Cause: usually the code was copied incompletely — the last lines got missed. Fix: copy it again with the copy button, not by selecting with the mouse.

3. It marks the wrong answer correct

Cause: the answer number counts from zero and it is off by one. Fix: count the choices starting at 0 and correct the number. This is the most common error in your own edits, not in the AI's work.

4. It looks plain and unstyled

Cause: the styling came as a separate file. Fix: "Put the styling inside the same HTML file so I only have one file to save."

When it breaks, continued

Two more, and a rule

5. It asks for a name or a login

Cause: the AI added a feature you did not ask for — it does this sometimes, trying to be helpful. Fix: "Remove the name field and any login. This must not collect anything about the student." Say it plainly; it will comply. And notice that this is the Tier 1 rule from page 4 doing its job.

6. It worked, then you changed something, and now it does not

Cause: a missing quote mark or comma in the question list. Fix: compare your edit to the pattern on page 10. Every question needs its quote marks closed and a comma between entries. If you cannot spot it, paste the question list into the chat and say "something in this list is broken — find it."

The rule that covers everything not on this list:
Describe what you saw, in ordinary words, and ask for it to be fixed. You do not need the right vocabulary. "The green thing does not appear when I get it right" is a completely serviceable bug report and it will be understood.

When to stop fixing and start over

If three rounds have not fixed it, the conversation has probably tied itself in a knot. Start a fresh chat, paste Prompt 1 again, and add the one thing that went wrong. This is faster than untangling, and it costs you two minutes.

Judgment

What not to build this way

Knowing where this stops is as valuable as knowing how to start. Four honest limits.

Anything that grades for real

This is practice, not assessment. It keeps no record, and it should not. A score that counts belongs in the system your district already uses, where it is backed up, auditable and covered by policy.

Anything holding a student's name

Page 4, Tier 3. The temptation arrives the moment the tool becomes genuinely useful. Keep the roster key somewhere else.

Anything open-ended

Essays, opinions, "explain your thinking" — a click-the-answer activity cannot judge those, and pretending otherwise teaches students that thinking has four options. Use this for the recall that frees your time for the open-ended work.

Anything you would not check yourself

If you would not read every question before handing it out on paper, do not hand it out on a screen either. The medium changed; your responsibility for the content did not.

The data rule in full

Tier 2: using real results safely

The activity in this pack collects nothing. But once you have built one tool, you will want one that looks at how students actually did. Here is how to do that without crossing a line.

What makes data de-identified

Not "I removed the last names." De-identified means a person who found this file could not work out which child it describes — even knowing your class. Student numbers, no names, no initials, no "the one with the IEP", no detail that only fits one child.

A worked example

Safe to put in a tool or a prompt:

S1: 8/10 S2: 6/10 S3: 9/10 S4: 4/10 Whole group missed Q3 and Q7 (both regrouping)

Not safe, even though it looks harmless:

Marcus: 4/10 - still struggling since his IEP meeting
Keep the key on paper. A sticky note in your planner that says S1 = Marcus does the whole job, and it never leaves your desk. The tool sees S1 forever.

The data rule in full

Tier 3: when to stop and ask

Sooner or later you will want to build something that genuinely needs real student information. That is not a failure of nerve — it is the point at which this stops being a solo project.

You are in Tier 3 the moment any of these is true

• Names, initials or student ID numbers go into the tool.
• It stores anything between sessions — a database, an account, a saved file of results.
• It lives on the public internet where a link would reach it.
• It holds grades that count, attendance, behavior records or anything from an IEP.
• Somebody other than you would need to log in to it.

What to do instead of abandoning the idea

Take it to your district's technology team as a proposal, not a fait accompli. Districts say yes to things reviewed in advance far more often than to things discovered in use. Bring what it does, what data it touches, and where it would live. Peninsula School District in Washington built its own tools this way and reports saving substantial money doing it — with the district involved, which is exactly the difference.

Why the district cares, in one sentence: student records are covered by federal and state law, and the school — not you personally — carries the obligation. A tool they reviewed is a tool they can stand behind.

Practical guidance, not legal advice. Your district's policy, your state's student privacy law, FERPA and COPPA are the authorities. Ask your district before real student information enters anything you build.

Before you build

Plan it first — five minutes with a pen

The teachers who get a genuinely useful tool are the ones who decided what it was for before they opened the chat. Fill this in.

What skill is this practicing?

One skill. Not a unit. "Rounding to the nearest ten", not "place value".

What are the three wrong answers your students actually give?

These become your distractors. Invented wrong answers teach nothing; the ones your class really produces teach a great deal.

For each one — what is the misconception behind it?

This is what you write in the feedback. It is the part no AI can do for you, and the part that makes the activity worth more than a worksheet.

Ten questions is plenty. A student who does ten with real feedback learns more than one who clicks through forty. Resist the urge to generate a hundred just because you can.
