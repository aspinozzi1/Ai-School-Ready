My first business idea

Every business starts the same way: somebody notices a thing people want, and makes it for them. Today that somebody is you. Answer with drawings or words — both count.

1 · What can I make or do that people would like?
Think of things you're already good at: drawing, baking with help, making bracelets, walking dogs, watering plants…
2 · Who would want it?
Real people you know: neighbors, grandparents, kids at co-op, the block party…
3 · What would I need to make it?
List the supplies. Circle the ones you already have.
4 · Draw your idea — or your sign!
5 · One sentence that sells it:
"The coldest lemonade on the hottest street." What's yours?

The grown-up coach page

Your child does the thinking; you do the typing. These AI prompts turn the worksheet into a conversation — run them, read the results together, and let your kid pick, reject, and improve. That editing is the entrepreneurship lesson.

Grow the idea (after question 1)

"A [AGE]-year-old wants to start a first tiny business and is good at [WHAT THEY WROTE]. Suggest five kid-run business ideas that use that skill, need under $10 of supplies, and can be sold to family and neighbors. One line each."

Play the customer (after question 5)

"Pretend you are a friendly neighbor at a [LEMONADE STAND / CRAFT TABLE]. I'll read you a kid's sales sentence; respond as the customer with one nice comment and one honest question a real customer would ask. Keep it gentle — the seller is [AGE]."

The safety frame (say it once, mean it always):

  • You hold the keyboard — chatbots are for 13 and up, and this stays true for business ideas too.
  • Your child's name, photo, school, and street never go into an AI tool — say "a 7-year-old," never who.
  • If anything ever goes online (a co-op sale post, a marketplace), the grown-up runs it, start to finish.
