1 · Does it touch a student's identity?A name, a diagnosis, a family situation, a story only one child fits

Delegate the draft, keep the judgment

The fastest teachers with AI aren't the ones using it for everything — they're the ones who decided once what it's for. Post the three questions; when a task passes, here's what delegating actually looks like.

Delegate the first draft

  • Letters and newsletters home
  • Leveling a passage up or down
  • Question banks, exit tickets, diagnostics
  • Rubrics a student can self-assess with
  • Sub plans and activity directions
  • Three versions of one task (scaffold / on-level / extension)
  • Icebreakers, brain breaks, station directions
  • Making your syllabus student-friendly

Never delegate

  • Anything with a student's name or story in it
  • Grades, feedback presented as yours but unread
  • IEP, 504, or placement decisions
  • Discipline calls and parent phone conversations
  • Seating charts and "who needs what" choices
  • What's worth teaching — the curriculum is a promise you make, not a prompt

What "delegate" means (and doesn't)

Delegating a draft means the AI produces the first version and you produce the final one. You'll keep maybe seventy percent, rewrite the rest in your voice, and catch the one thing that's wrong — still faster than a blank page. What it never means: sending an unread draft to a student or family. The read-through is non-negotiable.

The two rules that make every "yes" safe

Rule one
Never put student personally identifiable information into a public AI tool.
Describe the need, not the child.
Rule two: a human reviews everything before it reaches a student or family. AI is the intern; you're the teacher of record.
