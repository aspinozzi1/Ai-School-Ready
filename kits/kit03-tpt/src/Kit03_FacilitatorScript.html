How to use this script

Everything in plain type is meant to be spoken aloud. Read it verbatim (it's written for the ear) or glance and paraphrase once you're comfortable. Either way delivers a complete session. This kit assumes Kits 1 and 2: the one hard rule on student data and the four-part prompt formula are both in force here and get reused, not retaught. This session is hands-on from the middle onward, so read the lab support slides (22-28) before you present; they carry most of the troubleshooting weight so you don't have to improvise it live.

Meet Ms. Rivera. Throughout the deck you'll see the same example teacher's screen: Ms. Rivera, our running composite (not a real person; she appears in every kit). Today she builds one review game, start to finish, before the room tries it themselves. When her screen is up, point the room at it; it's the model to mimic. Her tracker chip sits top-right on every slide; the matching line under each slide cue below tells you what it says, so you always know what the room is reading.

From the founders

This note is from Adam and Katelyn Spinozzi, the two certified teachers who wrote this kit. It's here to give you their perspective before you present. Sharing it is entirely your call: read parts aloud where they fit, retell them in your own words, or keep it to yourself as background. The session is complete either way.

We're Adam and Katelyn, and here is why this session matters for what happens in your classrooms: every other kit in this series teaches teachers to use a tool someone else built. This one teaches them to build the tool. That is a different kind of confidence, and it is the one that sticks. A teacher who has watched a blank file become a working game they can hand to their own students stops seeing "AI can write code" as a headline about someone else's job and starts seeing it as something they did, once, with their own hands, on their own laptop.

The lessons we believe this hour will leave with your staff: the loop that makes any of this safe to attempt, describe, build, test, change one thing, which works whether the tool is a review game or the next thing your building needs; the honest limit that the tool can still misunderstand a vague request with total confidence, which is why you always test before you trust; and the recovery habit, saving a copy before a risky change, that turns every possible mistake in this lab into a thirty-second reset instead of a lost afternoon. Nobody in this room needs to become a programmer. They need to leave believing they already have the one skill that matters here: knowing how to describe, in plain language, exactly what they want.

Segment 2 · The rule, the term, and the loop (slides 5-8)

SLIDE 5The one hard rule0:04

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her one rule, before anything opens: no student data in any prompt, ever.

Before a single laptop opens, the rule from Kit 1, restated because today is hands-on and hands-on is where rules get forgotten: no student's name, score, grade, IEP status, behavior record, or writing sample ever enters a prompt. Not today, not with your own class, not as an example. Here's the good news: everything you build today is questions, answers, and a title. There is nowhere for a child's data to even go.

SLIDE 6What "vibe coding" actually means0:05

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her definition: describe what you want, in English, and test what comes back.

You've probably heard the term and maybe rolled your eyes a little. Here's what it actually means, no hype: you describe what you want, in plain English. The AI writes the code. You test what it made, and you keep describing changes until it does what you need. No programming class, no syntax to memorize. You already know how to describe what you want clearly, we worked on exactly that skill in Kit 2. That's the whole thing.

SLIDE 7What it is not0:06

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her correction: still her content, her questions, her class. The AI types.

Three honest corrections before we go further. It's not magic: every good result traces back to a clear, specific description, vague in still means broken out. It's not hands-off: you read every change, test every change, and decide what ships. And it's not "from scratch": today you change a working program by describing what you want. That's the real skill, and claiming otherwise would be a lie your students catch in a minute. We say what really happened, every time, in every product we make.

SLIDE 8The loop, named0:07

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her loop, named: describe, build, test, change one thing.

Four words that are the entire session: describe, build, test, change one thing. Describe what you want, specifically. It builds. You test it yourself, before anyone else does. You change one thing at a time and test again. That loop is what you're about to watch, then practice together, then run on your own for thirty minutes.

Segment 3 · Watch the loop, live (slides 9-13)

SLIDE 9Ms. Rivera builds one game, start to finish0:08

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her pick for today: a multiplication-facts review game, her own 4th graders.

Before you try this yourself, watch it happen once, live, on Ms. Rivera's screen. She's building a multiplication-facts review game for her own 4th graders, one artifact, the whole loop, start to finish. Nothing she does here is faster than what you'll do; watch the moves, not the speed.

SLIDE 10Describe what you want0:09

Her full chat window is the slide, so the tracker chip steps aside here.

Her first instinct was "make me a review game," which is too vague to act on. What she actually typed: she has a starter file, and she describes exactly what to replace, her eight questions pasted in full, a new title, and one more sentence that matters more than it looks: keep everything else in the file exactly the way it works now. That boundary line is the single most useful sentence in this whole session. It's what stops the AI from touching things you never asked it to touch.

SLIDE 11Build: it writes, she watches0:10

Her chat window again, no tracker chip.

Here's what comes back: the file, updated, her eight questions loaded and her new title on the opening screen. Name what just happened plainly: the tool changed one part of the file. It did not invent a new program, and it did not touch the parts she told it to leave alone. That's the entire "build" step, every time.

SLIDE 12Test: she plays it herself, first0:11

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her honest limit: it renamed a button she never asked it to touch.

She double-clicks the file. No install, no account, no wifi required. She answers a question wrong on purpose to check the feedback, and it works exactly right: the correct answer holds on screen and explains why. Then she finds an actual bug, a typo in one of her own answer choices. This is normal. This is what testing is for, and it's why the rule is: you play it before anyone else does. Every single time.

SLIDE 13Change one thing, then test again0:12

Her chat window one more time. This closes the loop; take it slowly.

Her fix: one sentence, naming exactly which answer choice and exactly what's wrong with it, nothing else. She runs it, plays the whole game again start to finish, and only then calls it done. Describe, build, test, change one thing, and you're back at test. That loop is the entire skill you're about to practice.

Segment 4 · Honest limits, live (slides 14-17)

SLIDE 14What it gets wrong, honestly0:14

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her honest limit: it renamed a button she never asked it to touch.

Before you try this yourselves, the honest limits. It can rename or move something you never asked it to touch. It can "fix" the wrong line if your request was ambiguous. And here's the one that matters most: it cannot tell when it has misunderstood you. It will sound just as confident either way. None of that is a reason not to build. It's the reason you always test before you trust, which you just watched Ms. Rivera do.

SLIDE 15A vague ask gets a vague fix0:15

Do this live, on the projector, right now, from your own account. Type exactly "make my game better" into your AI tool and let the room watch what comes back. This is scripted because it is the single most valuable ninety seconds in the session.

Watch what happens when I ask for something real but vague. I'm going to type "make my game better" and we'll see what comes back.

Let it respond. It will typically make a generic, harmless change to something that wasn't broken, plus a list of unsolicited suggestions. Read what it did out loud, then name what was missing, the way you'd coach a colleague.

Notice what happened: nothing is broken, but nothing useful happened either, because "better" isn't an instruction. Compare that to the fix we just watched: one sentence, one target, one test. That's the whole difference. A vague result isn't the tool failing, it's a teaching moment, and I wanted you to see it happen before you're the one typing.

If the live demo produces something odd rather than merely unhelpful: stay calm and name it out loud exactly like any other result: "Interesting, that's not what I expected either. This is exactly why we always test before we trust, and why you'll save a copy before your own risky changes in a few minutes." Undo or close without saving, and move to the next slide. An odd result here is not a failed demo; it's the same lesson, one slide early.
SLIDE 16Save a copy before you prompt0:16

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her habit now: save a copy before every prompt. Thirty seconds, no exceptions.

One habit that makes every mistake reversible: before any change you're not sure about, save a second copy of the file first. Thirty seconds, no exceptions. The single most likely real problem in today's lab is a change that rewrites more of the file than you expected and loses your questions. A saved copy turns that from a disaster into a thirty-second reset. Teach yourself this as a step, not a tip, starting with your very first change today.

SLIDE 17No child's data. Ever.0:17

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her rule slide, same as Kit 1: no child's name, score, or record in any prompt.

Last word before hands go on keyboards. No child's data, ever, not a name, a score, a grade, an IEP note, a behavior record, or a writing sample, not in your prompts today and not in your classroom tomorrow, including yours. This game holds questions, answers, and a title. There's nowhere for a child's data to go, and that's by design, not an accident.

Segment 5 · Guided practice, together (slides 18-20)

SLIDE 18Everyone makes the same move0:18

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her hand up first: one change, together, before anyone works alone.

Devices out, starter file open, six minutes. This is the last checkpoint before independent work; do not skip it even if the room is eager to move ahead.

Open your starter file, everyone, right now. We're going to make one small change together, on the board, projected, so I can catch anyone who's stuck before we split up into independent work.

SLIDE 19Change the destination name0:20

Her full prompt is the slide, no tracker chip. Read it word for word, then give the room three minutes and circulate.

Type this into your AI tool, exactly as written, with your own school mascot or a place from your own subject in the brackets: "Change the DESTINATION at the top of the file from 'Fort Verity' to [your choice]. Change nothing else." This is small and low-risk on purpose. The point is proving the loop works on your machine, not making a meaningful game yet.

SLIDE 20Raise a hand if it worked0:23

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her checkpoint: did the room's one change actually show up? Confirmed.

Open your file. Does the new name show up on the title screen? If yes, you have the whole loop, and everything in the lab is this, repeated on real content. If no, hands up; help is coming to you.

Do not advance to the lab slide until every raised hand has been resolved. A facilitator cannot debug forty screens at once once the lab opens; this is the moment to catch anyone stuck.

Segment 6 · The lab: build your game (slides 21-28)

SLIDE 21The lab: build your game0:24

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her lab, same as yours: the starter file, her questions, two real changes.

This slide and the six that follow it stay up as reference for the whole 30-minute lab; you do not need to advance every minute. Circulate constantly.

Thirty protected minutes, starting now. Most of you: Track 1, open the starter file, press Put my own questions in on the opening screen, fill in the form with your own questions, then make two real changes by describing them, one cosmetic, one behavioral. A few of you already know you want to start from nothing: that's Track 2, and it's a completely normal path, not a harder one, raise a hand and I'll get you the first prompt. One rule for everyone: no student information, ever, and save a copy before any change you're unsure about.

SLIDE 22Your three moves0:25

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her Track 1: open the file, press Put my own questions in, fill in the form.

Three moves, on screen the whole time you're working: open the starter file, press Put my own questions in on the opening screen and type your eight to ten questions into the form, then make your two changes by describing them, testing after each one. Last, press Save my game as a file and walk out with it. Nobody edits code to enter questions or to save the game.

SLIDE 23The setup screen, up close0:26

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her setup screen: three ways to fill it, and the one that drafts a round for her.

This is the best ninety seconds of the lab. Do it live on the projector. Open your own copy of the starter file, press Put my own questions in, type a topic into the What this round reviews box (use a subject, never a student), press Copy the prompt, and paste it into your AI tool. Let the room watch it answer.

Here is what the setup screen does. Press "Put my own questions in" on the opening screen and you get one page. Three ways to fill it. One: type your questions straight into the form, one card each. Two: paste a list, one question per line, and the page loads it and tells you any line it had to skip and why. Three, the fast one: type your topic into the top box and press "Copy the prompt." That prompt asks any AI tool for a whole round in the exact shape this page wants. Watch.

Paste the prompt into the AI tool. When the twelve lines come back, paste them into the box labeled Paste what the AI gave you here and press Load these questions. Then read the result out loud against what a teacher would actually want.

Twelve questions, in moments. That is fast. Fast is not the same as right, so now I read them the way you will. Look at this one [point at a real question from your round and say what is off: a wrong answer that is too easy to rule out, a fact that differs from what you teach, a "why" line that does not teach]. The AI drafted, I decide. Every one of you reads every question before anyone plays it, and fixes the ones that are off, right in the form.

Find something real to point at. Almost every round has at least one question that is too easy, too tricky, or slightly wrong on a fact. Name it specifically. If, by chance, the round is flawless, say so and read one "why" line aloud to check it teaches on its own; that is the other place these drafts fall short. Either way you have just shown the room the habit the whole session is built on.

If asked "what's the idea field for?" That's what lets the game bring a question you missed back later instead of just moving on. It's two or three words, like "fractions" or "capitals," and every question on the same concept should use the same words. The game works best with three or four ideas in a round.
If the AI's answer will not load: the page names every line it skipped and why (usually a stray heading, a number at the start of a line, or a missing part). Say: "That is the page telling us exactly what to ask for." Paste the message back into the AI and say "fix this," or delete the odd lines and load again. A skipped line in front of the room is a teaching gift, not a failed demo.
SLIDE 24One cosmetic. One behavioral.0:27

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her two prompts: one cosmetic, one behavioral, both hers to choose.

Two starting templates for your required changes, more on the handout. Notice both end the same way: "keep everything else the same." That boundary line is doing real work. Test after each change, not after both, same as the loop, one at a time.

SLIDE 25Three minutes: write four questions now0:28

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her three minutes: four questions, roughed out, good enough to start.

Call this out by name at lab start, not as a footnote. Expect roughly a third of the room to arrive without their questions written, per the prep guide's ask.

If you didn't bring questions, that is completely normal, take three minutes right now. Pick one thing your students need to know cold this unit, and write four questions on it, rough is fine, you'll refine them while the game runs. Or skip the writing: type your topic into the setup screen and let the prompt there draft a round for you, then read every question. And if that isn't enough: the starter file already ships with a working sample game. Nobody is stuck.

SLIDE 26For the few who want to build from zero0:29

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her Track 2 path: the one she didn't need, but some in her building will.

For Track 2: this is the full first prompt from your handout, ready to paste into any AI coding tool. Notice it asks for the same design rules baked in from the start, no logins, works offline, brings missed questions back, never a public leaderboard. I won't hover here; ask for help when you want it, not before.

SLIDE 27"It gave me a blank page."During the lab

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her fix for a blank page: read the error out loud to the AI, try again.

Leave this up as a reference; say the recovery line out loud the first time it happens in the room, then let the next person self-serve from the slide.

If your screen goes blank: that's almost always a small typo in the code the AI just wrote. Copy the exact error message from your browser, or just say "it's a blank page, nothing shows up," and paste that back to the AI. Say "fix this." It almost always can, and it's usually a quick fix.

SLIDE 28"It changed the wrong thing." / "My questions disappeared."During the lab

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her fix for a wrong change: undo, narrow the ask, try again.

If it rewrote more than you asked for, this is the single most likely real problem today, and it's exactly why we saved a copy first. Close the file without saving, reopen your saved copy, and try again with a narrower request, for example "change only the title text, nothing else in the file." If you didn't save a copy, press Put my own questions in and type or paste your questions back in rather than starting over. Either way: stay calm, this is never unrecoverable, and a live recovery in front of the room is more reassuring than a clean run would have been.
