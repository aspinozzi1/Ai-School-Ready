Build Your First Classroom Tool

Everything you need before you present. Read this once (about 8 minutes), run the prep checklist, and you are ready. The one task that cannot wait is the first item below.

Task one, the week before: check the AI tool from a staff machine, on the school network, in the room. Open the AI tool you will use, sign in, and type one sentence. A blocked or filtered tool is this session ending at minute four. If it is blocked, ask your technology contact now, not the morning of. Do the same for the starter file (task two below).

What this session is

Kits 1 and 2 taught staff to use AI safely and to prompt it well. This session has staff build something. Every teacher leaves with a working review game, made from their own questions, saved as a single file on their own device. It runs in a web browser, needs no account or install, and works with the wifi off. Nobody edits code to enter their questions or save their game: the starter file has a setup screen where teachers type their questions, paste a list, or let an AI tool draft a round, and a button that saves the finished game as their own file.

Staff also learn the loop that makes this safe to attempt (describe, build, test, change one thing), watch Ms. Rivera do it start to finish, and make two changes by describing them. We are honest about the limit: teachers change a working program by describing what they want. They do not write one from a blank file, and the session says so.

Who should present it

Any confident colleague. You do not need to code. The script is word for word, the deck matches it cue for cue, and the recovery lines for the three predictable breaks are written out (slides 27 and 28). A live AI mistake during the lab is a teaching gift, not a failure. Say what went wrong out loud, fix it in front of the room, and move on.

Your prep checklist (about 30 minutes, spread over the week)

  • Check the AI tool from a staff machine on the school network (5 min, the week before): as above. If your district has approved one tool, use that one and say so.
  • Test the starter file on the room's laptops (5 min): double-click Kit03_ReviewGameStarter.html. It should open in the browser and play. Press Put my own questions in and confirm the setup screen appears.
  • Get the starter file to every participant (5 min): email it, or put it in your shared drive. Every person needs their own copy on their own device before the session.
  • Ask staff to bring eight to ten review questions (2 min): send the one-line email below. Expect about a third to arrive without them; the script covers it.
  • Rehearse the two live moments (8 min): the vague prompt on slide 15 and the AI-drafts-a-round demo on slide 23. Run each once in your own account so nothing surprises you.
  • Print the handouts (3 min): Participant Handout, First 48 Hours, Exit Ticket, one of each per person.
  • Click through the deck (2 min): confirm all 34 slides display.
The staff email, ready to send: "Bring a charged laptop and eight to ten review questions on something your students need to know cold, each with a right answer and three wrong ones. You will leave with a working review game you built."

Room, tech & the wifi plan

You need a projector, a visible clock, and a working connection for the AI tool. The starter file itself needs no wifi. One wifi point and forty laptops: the AI tool is the only thing that needs the network, and only in short bursts. Use a queue plan: tell the room that AI-drafting is done in waves, table by table, about three minutes each, while everyone else types or pastes questions into the setup screen. If the connection drops entirely, the lab still works. Teachers type their questions into the form, and you run the two changes on the projector with the room dictating.

Timing discipline: the lab must start by 0:24. It is the session. Cut discussion before you ever cut the lab.

Facilitator FAQ

"What if the game does not open?"

Open it with a current web browser, not a word processor. Right-click the file and choose Open with, then a browser. If it still fails, pair that person with a neighbor for the lab.

"What if the AI drafts questions with wrong facts?"

Celebrate it. That is the lesson: fast is not the same as right. The teacher reads every question before it is played and fixes the ones that are off.

"What if someone wants to put student names or scores in the game?"

The one rule holds: no child's name, score, grade or record in any prompt or file. The game holds questions, answers and a title. It has nowhere for a child's data to go.

"Can our students play it?"

Yes. It is a single file. Share it the way your school shares any file, and check with your district about how it wants tools like this shared with students.

After the session: 5 minutes

  • Collect exit tickets; they double as PD documentation.
  • Ask three volunteers to email you their saved game file. That starts the sharing habit in the 30-day plan.
  • Note which recovery lines you used. That is your story for the first PLC follow-up.
