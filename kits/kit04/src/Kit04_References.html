References

APA style. The note beneath each entry says exactly where the kit uses it, so any claim can be traced to its source.

Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998). Inside the black box: Raising standards through classroom assessment. Phi Delta Kappan, 80(2), 139–148. https://kappanonline.org/inside-the-black-box-raising-standards-through-classroom-assessment/ Used for: formative assessment, resting on roughly 250 studies, raises achievement when teachers check understanding and adapt (slide 5; prep guide; handout).

Elkins, S., et al. (2023). How useful are educational questions generated by large language models? In Artificial Intelligence in Education (AIED 2023). Springer. https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-36336-8_83 Used for: teachers rated LLM-generated questions 97% topic-relevant with usefulness 3.6/4, with teacher vetting integral to the process (slide 12; handout; admin one-pager).

Gallup, & Walton Family Foundation. (2025). Teaching for tomorrow. https://news.gallup.com/poll/691967/three-teachers-weekly-saving-six-weeks-year.aspx Used for: 57% of AI-using teachers say AI improves the quality of their grading and student feedback (slide 17; admin one-pager).

Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). The power of feedback. Review of Educational Research, 77(1), 81–112. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/003465430298487 Used for: feedback averages a 0.79 effect size, among the most powerful influences on achievement, with impact varying widely by how it is given (slide 3; prep guide; admin one-pager).

Kaliisa, R., Misiejuk, K., López-Pernas, S., & Saqr, M. (2025). How does artificial intelligence compare to human feedback? A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01443410.2025.2553639 Used for: across 41 studies (4,813 students), no statistically significant difference in learning performance between AI-generated and human feedback; framed alongside Steiss et al. so neither half of the evidence is oversold (slide 13; prep guide FAQ).

Kaufman, J. H., Woo, A., Eagan, J., Lee, S., & Kassan, E. B. (2025). Uneven adoption of artificial intelligence tools among U.S. teachers and principals in the 2023–2024 school year (RR-A134-25). RAND Corporation. https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA134-25.html Used for: about one in five teachers used AI to plan instruction at all in 2023–24; among those, about a quarter used it to help assess students, most often building assessments, quizzes, and rubrics, which RAND lists as their second most common use after generating lesson materials (slide 3; prep guide; admin one-pager). Treating assessment drafting as high-leverage work to delegate is AI-Ready School's position, not a RAND finding.

Merrimack College, & EdWeek Research Center. (2022). How teachers spend their time: A breakdown. Education Week. https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/how-teachers-spend-their-time-a-breakdown/2022/04 Used for: teachers work about 54 hours a week, under half spent directly teaching, with a median 5 hours a week on grading and feedback (slide 2; slide 32; prep guide; admin one-pager).

Panadero, E., Jonsson, A., Pinedo, L., & Fernández-Castilla, B. (2023). Effects of rubrics on academic performance, self-regulated learning, and self-efficacy: A meta-analytic review. Educational Psychology Review, 35(4), 113. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09823-4 Used for: rubric use has a moderate positive effect on academic performance (g = 0.45), with smaller pooled effects on self-regulated learning (g = 0.23) and self-efficacy (g = 0.18) whose confidence intervals include zero (slide 6; handout; admin one-pager).

Steiss, J., et al. (2024). Comparing the quality of human and ChatGPT feedback of students' writing. Learning and Instruction, 91, 101894. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475224000215 Used for: well-trained humans gave higher-quality feedback than ChatGPT on nearly all dimensions; the basis for "AI is the floor, the teacher is the ceiling" (slide 13; handout; prep guide FAQ).

Further reading: five worth your time

  • Black & Wiliam, "Inside the Black Box" (linked above): still the most readable case for formative assessment; short enough for a PLC to read together.
  • Hattie & Timperley, "The Power of Feedback" (linked above): the most-cited treatment of what makes feedback land or misfire; worth reading before you draft a single comment starter.
  • Panadero et al.'s rubric meta-analysis (linked above): for the colleague who suspects rubrics are just paperwork; the evidence says otherwise.
  • Kaliisa et al.'s AI-vs-human feedback meta-analysis (linked above): the current state of the evidence, useful for anyone who wants the debate firsthand.
  • RAND's adoption report, RR-A134-25 (linked above): for leaders planning rollout; shows where AI use is growing and where it hasn't reached.
