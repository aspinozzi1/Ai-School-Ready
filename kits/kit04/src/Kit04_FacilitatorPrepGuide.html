AI for Assessment: Rubrics, Feedback, and Question Banks

Everything you need before you present. Read this once (about 8 minutes), run the 15-minute prep checklist, and you're ready.

What this session is

The session takes AI to the biggest pile on every teacher's desk: assessment. Staff learn what makes assessment materials effective (formative checks, readable rubrics, sharp questions), work through three scripted prompt workshops (rubrics, question banks, feedback starters), get the red lines in writing, and spend a 19-minute lab building a rubric and a question bank for a real assessment already on their calendar, plus three reusable feedback starters for their most common student patterns.

The stance: repeat it until it's furniture
AI drafts assessment materials. It never grades students, never sees student work with names attached, and never makes decisions about students. Feedback that reaches a student always passes through the teacher.

Why the stakes are worth it: teachers work about 54 hours a week, with a median 5 hours on grading and feedback (Merrimack/EdWeek, 2022), and feedback is among the most powerful influences on achievement we've measured (Hattie & Timperley). RAND found that about one in five teachers used AI to plan instruction at all in 2023–24, and about a quarter of those used it to help assess students. We treat assessment drafting as high-leverage work to delegate: the workload is real, and teacher review of every line stays explicit.

Who should present it

Same bar as Kits 1–3: any confident colleague. You do not need to be an assessment expert; the workshops are fully scripted, the evidence is quoted for you, and the honest-limits section is written to be read aloud. What you must hold firmly is the stance above. If a question pushes toward "couldn't AI just grade the quizzes?", the answer is a warm, immovable no, and the script gives you the words.

One new prep item matters most: the pre-session email. The lab only works if people arrive with a real upcoming assessment in mind and their notes (however messy) on what good work looks like. Send the one-liner in the checklist below at least two days ahead. Second small item: add an empty "Assessment" heading to the staff prompt doc from Kit 2, so lab templates have a home waiting.

Your 15-minute prep checklist

  • Send the staff email (2 min, two days ahead): "Bring a charged device, one real assessment you'll give in the next month (test, essay, project, practical), and any notes on what good work looks like. You'll leave with the rubric and the question bank."
  • Add an "Assessment" section to the staff prompt doc (1 min): just the heading; the lab fills it.
  • Skim the script once (6 min): the three workshops on slides 9–14 and the red lines on slides 15–16 are the heart; read those aloud once.
  • Click through the deck (2 min): confirm all 30 slides display.
  • Print the handouts (3 min): Participant Handout, First 48 Hours, Exit Ticket, one of each per person.
  • Bring one of your own rubrics (1 min): the local-example slot in the lab wants a real one from a real room.

Room, tech & timing

Same setup as Kits 1–3: projector, Wi-Fi, pair seating, a visible clock. Timing discipline: the lab must start by 0:30, and it is the longest lab in the series so far. Cut discussion before you ever cut the lab; the one thing you never cut is slides 15–16, the red lines. If Wi-Fi fails, run the rubric workshop as a whole-group sport on the projector using your phone's hotspot, and have pairs hand-vet the sample question bank in the handout.

Facilitator FAQ

"So is this auto-grading?"

No, and say it with a smile and a full stop. AI drafts materials: rubrics, questions, feedback language. Grading is reading real student work and making a judgment about a real kid, and that stays with the teacher, every time. This stance is on slide 15, in the handout, and on the admin one-pager, on purpose.

"Can I paste a student's paragraph in to get feedback help?"

Only an anonymous excerpt with zero identifying details, judged by Kit 1's test: no names, no classmates, no story details a neighbor could recognize. When unsure, don't paste; describe the pattern instead ("students keep confusing X with Y"). The script teaches this exact rule on slide 16.

"What about the AI essay-scoring tool our district is piloting?"

Stay factual and stay in scope: district-adopted platforms follow district policy and their own data agreements; today is about public, general-purpose tools and teacher-built materials. Park platform questions for leadership; don't endorse or criticize a product by name.

"What if someone says the research proves AI feedback is just as good?"

The honest-evidence framing on slide 13 handles it: trained humans beat ChatGPT on quality dimensions (Steiss et al., 2024), while a 41-study meta-analysis found no significant difference in pooled learning outcomes (Kaliisa et al., 2025). Both are real. Our red line isn't hung on effect sizes; it's hung on responsibility for students, which is not delegable.

After the session: 5 minutes

  • Collect exit tickets; they double as PD documentation.
  • Skim the "weak question I caught" answers; the best catches open PLC follow-up #2.
  • Check the staff doc's Assessment section has entries; nudge stragglers with one friendly line.
