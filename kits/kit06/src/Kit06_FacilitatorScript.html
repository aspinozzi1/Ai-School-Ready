How to use this script

Everything in plain type is meant to be spoken aloud. Read it verbatim (it's written for the ear) or glance and paraphrase once you're comfortable. Either way delivers a complete session. Slide cues, stage directions, and question boxes work exactly as they did in Kits 1 through 5. Run Kits 1 and 2 before this one: today leans on Kit 1's privacy rules and Kit 2's prompt formula and voice round.

Meet Ms. Rivera. Throughout the deck you'll see the same example teacher's screen: Ms. Rivera, our running composite (not a real person; she appears in every kit). When her screen is up, point the room at it; it's the model to mimic in the lab. If someone asks who she is, say exactly that: a fictional teacher we use across the series so you always see what the work looks like before you try it yourself. Her tracker chip sits top-right on every slide; the matching line under each slide cue below tells you what it says, so you always know what the room is reading.
The stance of this whole session
AI drafts the message; you make it yours. Families hear you, not the tool. Every message is reviewed and personalized before it sends, and hard news is never delivered by a draft: you call.

From the founders

This note is from Adam and Katelyn Spinozzi, the two certified teachers who wrote this kit. It's here to give you their perspective before you present. Sharing it is entirely your call: read parts aloud where they fit, retell them in your own words, or keep it to yourself as background. The session is complete either way.

We're Adam and Katelyn, and here is why this session matters for what happens in your classrooms: family communication is where trust is built or lost, and it lives almost entirely in teachers' unpaid margins. The research is blunt about the stakes: even one personal sentence home per week measurably changes what happens for kids. But the messages that matter most are the ones teachers have the least time to write, and the ones that go out tired, rushed, or angry are the ones that cost the most. AI can buy that time back, on one condition: the voice that comes through it stays yours.

The lessons we believe this hour will leave with your staff: how to draft family messages in minutes instead of evenings and still sound like themselves; how to make the positive note home a weekly habit instead of a good intention; how to write in plain language so every family can actually use the message; how to repair the tone of a hard message before it costs a relationship; and the line that keeps all of it human, that hard news is delivered by a person, by voice, every time. Trust is built one reviewed, personal message at a time. This hour buys the time; the trust stays yours to earn.

Segment 1 · Welcome & why messages matter (slides 1–4)

SLIDE 1Title0:00

Slide 1 up as people arrive. Start on time.

Welcome back. Today is about the part of teaching nobody trained us for and everybody gets judged on: what we send home. Newsletters, updates, the email you rewrite four times because the first three sounded wrong. By the end of this hour, next week's real message will be drafted, sounding like you, and scheduled to send; you'll have a positive-note habit ready to start; and the message itself will be written plainly enough that every family on your roster can actually use it. That's the hour.

SLIDE 2The margin problem0:01

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her math: one wave of messages a week, drafted in the margins after dinner.

First the honest picture. Teachers work about 54 hours a week, and less than half of it is direct teaching; the rest is where communication lives, usually in the after-dinner margins. And teachers who've made AI a weekly habit report saving nearly six hours a week, with drafting communication among the most common uses. So the time is real and recoverable. The question of this session is how to recover it without families noticing a robot moved in.

SLIDE 3One sentence moves the needle0:03

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her lever: one sentence home per week moved real outcomes. She wants that at scale.

Now the reason this hour matters beyond your inbox. In a field experiment, Harvard researchers Kraft and Rogers had teachers send one individualized sentence home per week in a summer program for students retaking a course they had failed. One sentence. The share of students failing to earn the credit fell from about 16 percent to about 9 percent, a 41 percent reduction, and the biggest effects came from messages about what the student could improve. One personal sentence, once a week. The barrier was never that teachers didn't care; it was that personal-at-scale cost hours nobody had. That's exactly the cost AI just changed.

SLIDE 4Agenda + one promise0:04

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her goal: next week's newsletter from blank page to scheduled send, and the batch habit started.

The hour: what AI is genuinely good at in family communication and the one thing it threatens, your voice. Then three makeovers on Ms. Rivera's screen: the newsletter, the difficult email, and the positive-note batch. Then the tone-repair kit and the lines that never move. Then the lab: you take the one real message you owe families this week from blank page to scheduled send: drafted, voiced, the hard sentence repaired, action items on top. That's the promise: you leave with a real message ready to send, not notes about messages.

Segment 2 · The tool and the voice (slides 5–8)

SLIDE 5What it's good at here0:05

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her shortlist: newsletters, updates, tone repair, reading level, reformatting.

Where the intern earns its keep in communication: first drafts of anything routine, newsletters, updates, reminders; repairing tone when you're too tired or too angry to find it yourself; adjusting reading level so a message lands for every family; and reformatting, the same update as an email, a text blurb, and a printable flyer. Notice what's not on the list: anything that requires knowing the family. That part was never draftable.

SLIDE 6The voice problem0:07

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her worry, named: if every note sounds like every other note, families notice.

Now the threat, named honestly. AI output has a house style: smooth, pleasant, beige. In a lesson plan, beige is fine. In a message to a family who knows you, beige is noticeable, and across a school it compounds: if every teacher's newsletter sounds like the same cheerful robot, families stop reading all of them. Your voice, the slightly imperfect, recognizable, human one, is not a flaw to polish out. It's the asset. Kit 2's voice round is about to become the most important skill in this session.

SLIDE 7The two rules ride along0:09

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her two rules, still on: the situation, never the child; nothing goes out unread.

The permanent rules, pointed at communication. Rule one, from Kit 1: no student personally identifiable information in a public AI tool, and family communication is where the temptation is strongest, because the message IS about a specific child. The move is the same as always: describe the situation to the tool, "a middle schooler with several missing assignments," and add the name after the draft is back in your own email. Rule two: nothing AI-drafted reaches a family unread. Your name is on it; a family can't tell what you wrote from what you approved, and they shouldn't have to.

SLIDE 8The banner0:11

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her banner: families hear HER, not the tool.

So here's the banner over everything today: families hear you, not the tool. AI drafts; you review, personalize, and send. If a message wouldn't survive you reading it aloud in your own voice at a conference, it isn't done. Hold that; every technique in the next twenty minutes serves it.

Segment 3 · Three makeovers (slides 9–12)

SLIDE 9Makeover #1: the newsletter0:12

Makeover one, on Ms. Rivera's screen, and count the four parts with me: role in teal, a warm, organized teacher. Task in navy: this week's family newsletter. Context in amber, and look how much she gives it: the fractions unit wrapped, science fair Thursday at six in the gym, picture day Friday, blue permission slip due Wednesday. Format in green: under 150 words, skimmable on a phone, action items bulleted, one positive line about the class's week. That's Kit 2's formula doing family work. Ninety seconds of typing, and the draft comes back needing only her voice pass.

SLIDE 10Makeover #2: the difficult email0:15

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her difficult email: vent draft deleted, professional draft sent, relationship intact.

Makeover two: the email you shouldn't send. A parent emails something unfair at 9 p.m. and your first draft is honest in exactly the way careers end. Here's the move: let the tool absorb the heat. Describe the situation, no names: "Draft a calm, professional reply to a frustrated parent who believes their child's grade is unfair. Acknowledge the frustration, explain that specifics belong in a conversation, offer two meeting times, keep it under 120 words, firm and warm." The draft comes back with the emotion out and the relationship intact. You add the specifics in your own email, where names are safe. And the companion habit, the 24-hour rule: if the message made you angry, the reply waits a day unless safety can't. No AI needed for that one; it's just wisdom with a timestamp.

SLIDE 11Makeover #3: the positive-note batch0:18

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her batch: three one-sentence positive notes, each personalized before sending.

Makeover three is the Kraft and Rogers finding turned into a habit. The highest-leverage message in the research is one individualized sentence, and the version focused on what a student can improve moved outcomes most. So: the batch. Once a week, ask the tool for a page of one-sentence note starters, improvement-focused and warm, organized by situation: "showed persistence on a hard problem," "helped a classmate unprompted," "turned a rough Monday around." No names anywhere near the tool. Then you spend five minutes matching starters to real kids, personalizing each one in your own words, in your own email. The tool made the blank page cheap; the sentence that lands is still yours. Today's lab takes one message start to finish, so the batch is homework with training wheels: the exact prompt is on your handout, and it's Send 2 on the First 48 Hours sheet.

SLIDE 12Practice: diagnose the message0:21

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her diagnosis eye: a bad message is a prompt missing a part. Role, Context, or Format: name it.

Interactive. Read each sample, let the room name the missing formula part, confirm with the answer strip at the bottom of the slide. 45-min cut: samples 1 and 3 only.

Quick practice, and it's really Kit 2 practice. When a message home goes wrong, it's usually because the prompt skipped part of the formula: Role, Task, Context, Format. So don't just call the symptom; diagnose which part got skipped. One: a 400-word newsletter where the field-trip permission slip sits in the last paragraph, so the key request is buried at the bottom. That's missing Format: the prompt never said "action items first, under 150 words." Two: a note that reads "formative assessment data indicates," sent to families. Missing Context: the prompt never said who's reading, families skimming on phones, plain words. Three: a message with three updates and nothing for families to do. Missing Format again: the prompt never asked for an action item with a date. Four: a perfectly polished email that sounds like a bank wrote it. Missing Role: the prompt never said "a warm teacher who sounds like me." The fix is never mysterious: hand the prompt the part it skipped, and run it again.

Segment 4 · Tone, and the lines that hold (slides 13–17)

SLIDE 13The tone-repair kit0:24

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her tone kit: 'warmer, shorter, plainer,' the read-aloud test, the 24-hour rule.

The tone-repair kit, small enough to memorize. Three commands fix ninety percent of drafts: warmer, shorter, plainer. One test catches the rest: read it aloud; your ear knows your voice better than your eyes do. And one rule protects your career: the 24-hour rule for anything written angry. That's the whole kit. It fits on the handout's back pocket and it will save you a hundred small regrets a year. Let's put it to work right now.

SLIDE 14Practice: call the repair0:26

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her repair calls: cold gets 'warmer,' rambling gets 'shorter,' jargon gets 'plainer.'

Interactive. Read each draft, let the room call the command, then read the repaired line. 45-min cut: walk draft A only.

Three real drafts, one command each; call it out. Draft A: "Reminder: the permission slip was due Monday. Students without one cannot attend." Every word is accurate and the whole thing lands like a citation. The call is warmer: "We're missing a few permission slips. Send yours back by tomorrow and everyone's on the bus." Draft B: sixty words of throat-clearing to say picture day is Friday. The call is shorter: "Picture day is Friday. Smiles optional, forms required." Draft C: "Our formative assessment data indicates additional at-home numeracy reinforcement would benefit your student." The call is plainer: "Your child is still working on multiplication. Ten minutes of practice at home this week would help." One command each, and notice how fast the repair comes back. That speed is why there's no excuse left for sending the cold version.

SLIDE 15One red line: translation0:28

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her red line: she writes it plain; she doesn't ship what she can't read.

One red line before the lab, and it's short. We do not use public AI tools to translate messages to families. Not because the tools are useless: because you cannot review what you cannot read, and nothing goes home with your name on it unreviewed. That rule doesn't bend for a language. Official and legal documents already have a lane, your district's translation channels; that's their obligation and their job. Your job is the original: plain language, short sentences, no idioms. A plain-language message is the version every family can actually use, including multilingual families, and it's the version district translators do their best work from. Write it plain; don't ship what you can't read.

SLIDE 16The human-only list0:29

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her human-only list: safety, discipline, grief, IEP news. She calls first.

And the line that never moves. Some news is never delivered by a draft: anything touching a child's safety, a serious discipline situation, a family's grief, or special-education decisions. For those, the technology is a telephone and the script is your own heart, first. A written follow-up can come later, and even then you write it yourself. If a message would change how a family sees their child, it deserves a human voice. No exceptions, and say so out loud to your staff, because this is the line that makes all the efficiency defensible.

SLIDE 17What this builds0:30

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her trust ledger: every reviewed, personal message is a deposit.

One beat on what all this builds. Every message home is a deposit in or a withdrawal from the only account that matters when something goes wrong: trust. Reviewed, personal, on-time messages, written plainly enough that every family can use them, are deposits. Late, generic, robotic ones are withdrawals. AI just made deposits cheap. The lab starts now.

Segment 5 · The lab: one real message (slides 18–28)

SLIDE 18Lab setup0:31

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her lab plan: the Friday newsletter, from blank page to scheduled send.

Dark slide. Devices out, pairs formed inside two minutes. Announce the tool.

Lab time, eighteen minutes, one deliverable, completely real: the message you actually owe families this week, taken from blank page to scheduled send. Drafted, voiced, the hard part repaired, action items on top. Ms. Rivera is doing hers on screen, her Friday newsletter; you'll watch that same message evolve at every step, and yours evolves alongside it. Ground rules: no student information of any kind, no family names; describe situations, never people. Pair up; every message gets a second set of ears before it counts as done. Go.

SLIDE 19Lab step 1: draft the real message0:32

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her raw material: five fragments on a sticky note, and twenty minutes.

6 minutes. Circulate. Weak drafts lack context; nudge with "what does a family actually need to know and do this week?" 45-min cut: 5 minutes.

Step one, six minutes: draft it. The message you actually owe families: newsletter, unit update, event reminder, whatever is real. All four parts, and be generous with context: what happened, what's coming, what families need to do and by when. Ms. Rivera is doing one artifact all the way through this lab, her Friday newsletter, and the next three slides are her whole step one in order: the notes she starts from, the prompt she types, and the draft that comes back. If you ever feel lost, copy her structure exactly.

SLIDE 20What she starts from0:33

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her starting point, in full: the Friday notes she has to turn into a newsletter.

Leave this up while pairs write their own raw material. Read the sticky note aloud in a hurried voice; the room will recognize its own Friday.

This is where she actually starts, and it is nothing fancy. Friday, 2:40 in the afternoon, buses in twenty minutes, and the newsletter goes out at 3:00. What she has is five fragments on a sticky note: fractions unit done, they finally got equivalent fractions; science fair Thursday at six in the gym; picture day Friday; blue slips still missing a bunch, due Wednesday; say something nice, they worked hard. No greeting, no order, no sentences. That's the blank page. And here's the rule that runs through the rest of this hour: every fact in the message she finally sends comes off this note. The tool supplies the shape. It never supplies the facts.

SLIDE 21The prompt she types0:34

Her full chat window; no tracker chip on this slide. Point at the colors as you read them.

And this is what she typed, with those notes pasted underneath. Same four parts as slide 9, aimed at her real week. Role, in teal: "You are me, a warm, organized teacher writing to families I see every day." Task, in navy: "Turn the notes below into this week's family newsletter." Context, in amber, and this is the part people skip: every fact off the sticky note, plus the sentence that saves her the tone repair later, "nobody should feel scolded about the slip." Format, in green: under 150 words, action items first with dates, one warm line about the week, plain and easy to read on a phone. At worst, copy that shape with your own notes and your step one is done.

SLIDE 22The draft that came back0:35

Read the draft aloud in a flat voice, then stop. Let the room hear the beige before you name it.

Four seconds later, this comes back. Read it off the screen with me: "Hello families! We had a wonderful week concluding our fractions unit. The students demonstrated excellent growth…" Every fact in it is right, because every fact came off her note. Two things are wrong. It isn't her: "demonstrated excellent growth" and "please be advised" are nobody's sentences. And the one thing families actually have to do is sitting in the last line. That's a normal first draft, including the beige one on your own screen right now. The next three steps are how it becomes hers.

SLIDE 23Lab step 2: the voice pass0:38

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her newsletter, v2: it sounds like her now. The slip is still buried.

4 minutes. Pairs read their own drafts ALOUD; the partner's ear is the voice test. 45-min cut: fold step 4 in here: one read-aloud fixes voice and order together.

Step two, four minutes, the voice pass. Read your draft aloud to your partner. Every sentence your mouth wouldn't say, retype it the way you'd say it, or ask for warmer, shorter, plainer. On screen, Ms. Rivera's v2 of the same newsletter: "demonstrated excellent growth" became "I was proud of how your kids stuck with the tricky parts." It sounds like her now. One thing is still wrong with it, and that's the next step.

SLIDE 24Her iterations: what she typed next0:40

Her full chat window; no tracker chip. Read the right-hand column aloud; that column is the teaching.

While you work, here is her whole thread: one chat, nothing re-pasted, four follow-ups, and each one a move you already have. "Rewrite it the way I would say it out loud, and lose 'demonstrated excellent growth'": beige became hers. "Rewrite the permission-slip line warm and guilt-free, ending with what to do and by when": the nag became an invitation, same deadline, no sting. "Move everything families have to do to the top, as three bullets with dates": a parent skimming at nine at night now sees all three in the first four lines. "Plainer, and under 150 words; say science fair, not culminating event": the throat-clearing is gone and it fits on one phone screen. Six minutes, four follow-ups. Iteration is not failure; it's the work.

SLIDE 25Lab step 3: the hard sentence0:42

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her newsletter, v3: the permission-slip nag rewritten warm, plain, and guilt-free.

4 minutes. This is the deep-practice step; protect it. Circulate for the sentence people are avoiding.

Step three, four minutes: the hard sentence. Almost every real message carries one item you'd rather not write: the fee reminder, the missing slips, the gentle nag. Find yours and rewrite it warm, plain, and guilt-free, ending with exactly what to do and by when. Ms. Rivera's was the permission-slip line. Before: "Please be advised that students without a signed permission slip will be unable to participate." After: "If the blue slip hasn't made it home yet, no worries: it happens. Send it back by Wednesday so nobody misses out. Need a fresh copy? Just reply." Same requirement, zero sting, and the deadline still does its job. If your hard sentence came out a little angry, this is where the 24-hour rule gets its workout.

SLIDE 26Lab step 4: the access pass & sign-off0:46

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her newsletter, final: action items on top, plain words, scheduled for Friday at 3:00.

4 minutes. Partners swap for the final read. 45-min cut: folded into step 2.

Last step, four minutes: the access pass, then the sign-off. Pull every action item to the top as a short list with dates. Plain words, under about 150. Then trade with your partner one last time: they read it as a tired parent on a phone at 9 p.m. and flag anything unclear. When it comes back clean, schedule the send: pick the day and the time right now. On screen, the three things the pass changed for Ms. Rivera: action items to the top, three bullets with the slip first and every date attached; plain words, so "culminating activity" became "science fair"; and the one thing her partner caught, that "Thursday at 6" could be read as six in the morning, so it now reads "Thursday, 6:00 p.m." Scheduled for Friday at 3:00. That message is done, and done means it sends.

SLIDE 27The finished message, word for word0:49

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her finished newsletter, word for word: what a family opens on Friday at 3:00.

Read it exactly as a family would read it on a phone. This is the payoff slide of the hour; don't rush it.

And here is the whole thing, word for word, the way it lands in a family's inbox on Friday at 3:00. Subject line: "This week in Room 12: three quick things." "Hi families, happy Friday! Three quick things. Blue permission slip: back by Wednesday. Need a fresh copy? Just reply. Science fair: Thursday, 6:00 p.m., in the gym. Picture day: Friday. We finished fractions this week, and I was proud of how your kids stuck with the tricky parts. See you Thursday, Ms. Rivera." Three things worth pointing at. Everything families have to do is in the first four lines, with dates. The permission slip asks instead of scolds. And the last sentence is hers, not the tool's; no draft was ever going to produce it, because no draft was in the room when those kids stuck with the tricky parts. Eighteen minutes, one message, every fact off the sticky note, and she read every word before it went. That's the standard for everything any of us sends home.

SLIDE 28Share-out0:50

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her share-out: v1's opening read against the final. Same facts; now it's her.

3 voices, one minute each. 45-min cut: two voices.

Three voices, a minute each: read us your before and your after, the first draft against the version you just scheduled. Listen for how different the voices sound; that's the whole point. Same tool, thirty different teachers, thirty recognizable humans.

SLIDE 30Honest limits0:54

MS. RIVERA'S SCREEN · SO FAR   Her honest limits: AI can't know the family or repair trust it didn't build.

Honest limits, as always. The tool doesn't know your families, their histories, or what last year did to their trust in schools; you do, and personalizing is where that knowledge enters. The tool also sounds fluent in every language, and that's exactly why translation stays off your desk: fluent-sounding and faithful are different things, and you can't check the difference in a language you don't read. And no tool repairs trust; only consistency does. AI lowers the cost of consistency. That's its whole contribution, and it's a big one.
