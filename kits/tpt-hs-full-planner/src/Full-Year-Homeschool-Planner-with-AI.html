The AI planning-prompt library

The year-overview prompt

"Suggest 12 monthly themes for a [GRADE-LEVEL] homeschool year covering [SUBJECTS/INTERESTS], loosely tied to the seasons where it makes sense. One line per month explaining the idea."

The monthly-theme starter

"Build a month of activity ideas around [THEME] for a [AGE] learner: one main project, three living-book style read-aloud suggestions, and two field-trip ideas near [REGION, no address]."

The re-plan-when-life-happens prompt

"We lost two weeks to [REASON, e.g. 'illness']. Given our year theme was [THEME] and we still need to cover [REMAINING TOPICS], suggest how to compress the rest of the term without cutting corners on [PRIORITY SUBJECT]."

The family data-safety habit

Every prompt above describes the learner, never the child — no names, ages tied to a name, or identifying details. And the grown-up always holds the keyboard: AI drafts ideas for you to adapt, never a conversation your child has alone.

Quarterly review checkpoints

Four short check-ins beat one overwhelming June review — pencil in a date for each.

Quarter 1 review — did the theme match how the year actually started?
Quarter 2 review — what's ahead of schedule, what's behind?
Quarter 3 review — any subject due for the re-plan prompt?
Quarter 4 review — what carries into next year's plan?

Make it a rhythm, not a rulebook

The grid on page one is a plan, not a promise — real weeks run short on sleep, long on illness, and off-script more often than not. The point of a year-at-a-glance page isn't to follow it exactly; it's to know, at a glance, how far off you actually are, and to have a system for closing that gap without guilt.
