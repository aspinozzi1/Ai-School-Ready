## (a) THE DIRECTIVE QUESTION, DECIDED HERE BECAUSE IT HAD TO BE

Wednesday's worksheet asked the owner whether the **2026-09-23 product-shape
directive** — *"if it is extremely detailed, teachers won't want it; they want
something that is immediately actionable"* — applies to Lane B as well as to
Lane A. **It has not been answered, and Friday cannot wait for it.**

**Decision for this build, and an owner instruction overrules it:** keep the
unit format, adopt the mechanisms.

- **Keep** the five-day arc and the printable student pages. A Lane B buyer is
  planning a *unit*, not solving Monday. Twenty-odd pages is mid-range for that
  shelf, and build 1 at 23 pages is on the short side of it, not the long side.
  Cutting a unit to eight pages makes it worse, not more actionable.
- **Adopt** the two things from the new shape that cost nothing and fix the
  real failure. They are not about page count:
  1. **The shipped app must have a setup screen.** The class's products, prices
     and stock go in through a form inside the app. **No teacher edits code**,
     ever — that is the step where a Lane A product lost people, and in a
     classroom with thirty children watching it is worse.
  2. **The app ships an AI drafting prompt**, copy-and-paste, that turns the
     class's feature list into the starting product data in about thirty
     seconds, in a format the file parses on paste. Always with *read it before
     you use it.*

That is the honest reading: the directive was about **where the friction is**,
not about page count, and the friction in Lane B is the same one.

## (b) THE BUILD — The Class Store

**Lane B build 2 in the spec:** *market day, but the storefront is software the
class specced.*

### Title, ≤80 characters

**`Vibe Coding for Kids | The Class Store Your Students Design and You Build`** (73)

### Why this is a real step up and not build 1 in a costume

Say this in the pack explicitly, because it is the reason somebody who bought
build 1 buys this one.

**Build 1's app had to be useful. Build 2's app has to be right.**

A lost-and-found tool that is slightly wrong is still slightly useful. A
storefront that miscounts stock or mis-totals a cart is **visibly broken in
front of customers**, and the customers are the class. So the new idea is
correctness under other people's choices:

- The app must agree with the cash box. If the ledger says 14 and the tin says
  12, the software is wrong and somebody has to find out why.
- Stock has to go down when something sells, and the last one has to stop
  selling. Every class discovers this by selling eleven of ten pencils.
- **The class does the arithmetic by hand first**, on paper, before the app does
  it. Then they check the app against their own numbers. That is the lesson:
  *you cannot tell whether software is right unless you already know the
  answer.*

That single sentence is what build 2 teaches and build 1 did not.

### The design law, unchanged and not a compromise

**The grown-up holds the keyboard.** Chatbots are 13+, and no child's identity
enters a prompt. Students do the market research, the pricing, the feature
list, the testing, the selling and the iteration calls. The teacher does the
prompting and the building, projected so the class watches their own spec
become software.

**External corroboration worth one line in the pack** (found 09-23): Teaching
Entrepreneurship's 2026 AI lesson plan lands on the same rule independently —
AI belongs in an entrepreneurship class *after* the student has something to
think with, and the student keeps the problem choice, the evidence and the
judgment. It answers "you are letting AI do the thinking" before a buyer raises
it.

### The five-day arc

| Day | Students do | Teacher does |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · What will actually sell** | Survey the class. Rank what people want against what they will pay. Not "ideas" — evidence. | Model a bad survey question, then a good one. |
| **2 · Price it and stock it** | Set prices and opening stock. Work out by hand what a full sell-out earns. | Push on pricing. "Because it's cool" is not a reason. |
| **3 · Build it live** | Read the spec aloud, watch it become a storefront, call out what is wrong. | Prompt on the board. Let it fail and get fixed in front of them. |
| **4 · Open the store** | Run a real session. Serve customers. Record what breaks. | Say nothing. Write down every workaround you see. |
| **5 · Reconcile and change one thing** | **Count the tin against the ledger.** Find the gap, explain it, decide the one change. | Make that change live. Ship it. |

**Day 5 is the one that makes the unit.** Every other classroom-store lesson
ends at "we had a store." This one ends at *the numbers did not match and we
found out why*, which is the actual skill.

### The shelf model the app must support, and it is not invented

Classroom-store practice converges on **three price tiers**: free privileges,
cheap consumables (stickers, pencils), and an anchor prize students save for
over weeks. Build the product model around those three tiers rather than a flat
list, because a store with no anchor prize gets emptied on day one and stops
motivating anybody. That is practitioner consensus, **not a TPT row**, and the
pack should not present it as research.

### What the app must do, so the factory can test it rather than guess

- Opens by double-click. One HTML file. No accounts for anybody, works offline.
- **Setup screen**: products with name, price, opening stock and tier; the
  class currency's name; the store's name. No code touched.
- **The AI drafting prompt**, on that screen, turning a feature list into
  starting product data in a format the file parses on paste.
- Sell: tap items into a cart, see a running total, complete a sale.
- **Stock decreases on sale and the last unit stops selling** — with a clear
  message, not a silent failure.
- A running ledger: what sold, how many, total taken. **Reconciliation view for
  day 5**: expected total against counted total, and the difference.
- Save the whole store as the teacher's own file, so it survives a browser wipe
  and reopens next term.
- Print a price list for the shelf.
- **Nothing anywhere to type a child's name.** See below.

**Test it in a browser before shipping** — drive it with playwright, sell to
a sell-out, confirm the last unit refuses, reconcile with a deliberate
discrepancy, save and reopen the file, and report zero console errors.

## (d) PRICING — $18

Same as build 1, and the reason matters: **this is a comparable unit, not a
bigger one.** Raising the price because it is newer is how a store teaches its
buyers to wait for a sale. Licenses exactly half at **$9**.

### The competitor, named honestly, because it is free

**ClassBank is a free digital classroom economy for K-12** — students earn
classroom money, and there is a digital store to spend it in. LiveSchool and
PBIS Rewards sit in the same space. **Do not claim to undercut free.** The
difference is the one this whole catalog runs on:

- **Their store holds accounts for named children on a vendor's server.** Ours
  holds products and totals on the teacher's own device and transmits nothing.
- **Theirs arrives finished. Ours is the one the class designed**, which is the
  entire pedagogical point — the students cannot learn specification from
  software somebody else specced.

Both of those are true and defensible. "Better than ClassBank" is not, and a
buyer will say so in a review.

## (e) THE DATA RULE, AND THIS BUILD NEEDS IT MORE THAN ANY LANE B PRODUCT SO FAR

The tier is decided by **where the data lives and who can reach it**, not by
whether names appear. A store is the first Lane B build that tempts a teacher
straight into tier 3, and the pack must head it off.

- **Tier 1 — build freely.** Products, prices, stock, tiers, the store's name,
  the price list, the class totals. None of it is about a child.
- **Tier 2 — the teacher's own device.** The saved store file and the sales
  ledger, kept where they keep their own work, transmitted nowhere.
- **Tier 3 — stop.** **Who bought what.** A purchase history attached to an
  identified child is a behavior-and-spending record, and it is the obvious
  next feature every class will ask for. **The app refuses it and says why**,
  the same way the games refuse a named leaderboard. Also tier 3: balances per
  named student, and any of it synced, emailed or pasted into a chatbot.

**The app therefore tracks stock and totals, never buyers.** If a teacher
insists on balances, the honest answer in the pack is a paper ledger they keep
themselves, not a feature we build.

**And one thing that is not our call at all:** if real money is involved rather
than classroom currency, that is school-funds handling and it goes to the
office. The pack says so in one sentence and does not advise on it.

## (f) THE FAILURE MODES SPECIFIC TO THIS BUILD

1. **Selling eleven of ten pencils.** The single most likely bug, and the pack
   should let the class *find* it on day 4 rather than pre-empt it — then fix
   it on day 5. It is the best teaching moment in the unit.
2. **The AI builds a cart that recalculates the total from scratch each tap**
   and drifts on rounding. Prices in whole units of class currency avoid it.
   Say so in the prompt.
3. **Clearing browsing data wipes the store mid-term.** Hence save-to-file, and
   hence it is a task and not a tip.
4. **One laptop, thirty customers.** Day 4 needs a queue plan; the pack gives
   one rather than leaving the teacher to discover it live.
5. **The store empties on day one** because there is no anchor prize. The
   three-tier shelf exists to prevent it.
6. **A child pays and the software crashes.** The pack needs the recovery line:
   the paper ledger runs alongside on day 4 precisely so the class can
   reconcile on day 5.
