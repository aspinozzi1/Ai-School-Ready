**Contents (~14 pages):**
1. Cover + how to use
2. Goal-at-a-glance page (one goal per sheet: goal text, baseline, target, criterion)
3. Weekly data sheet — 12 weeks, trials/percentage columns
4. Daily quick-tally sheet (for goals measured per session)
5. Graph page — hand-plotted, gridded, with target line
6. Multi-goal roster overview (student numbers, not names)
7. "Is this goal on pace?" printable decision table — the zero-technology half
8. **AI Progress Summary page** — the differentiator

10. Meeting-prep one-pager (what the data says, in plain sentences)
11–13. Blank/editable variants of the core sheets
14. How this is built + what we are not claiming

**The AI page, concretely.** The prompt takes six weeks of de-identified
scores and returns a trend read, an on-pace judgment, and a *drafted
progress-report sentence*. That last item is the chore SPED teachers
dread most and nothing in this niche does it. Example shape:
"A 3rd grader's goal is 8/10 on two-digit addition with regrouping.
Weekly scores: 3, 4, 4, 6, 5, 7. Summarise the trend, say whether it is
on pace to meet the goal by June, and draft one progress-report sentence."

**GUARDRAILS — binding, and stated on the product:**
- This is a **tracking tool**. It never advises what a goal should say,
  whether a student qualifies for services, or what placement is
  appropriate. Those are IEP-team decisions.
- De-identified only: student numbers, grade, goal area, scores. Never a
  name, never a full roster, never a diagnosis in a prompt.
- Legal note: "informational; follow your district's IEP procedures and
  your team's guidance."
- Say plainly what it is not: not an IEP writer, not a compliance
  document, not legal advice.
- Audit any example data printed on the page: tally it and confirm the
  stated conclusion actually follows.

## THE PRIVACY REQUIREMENT — binding, and the hardest one yet

A completed at-a-glance sheet holds a **named student's confidential
special education record**: disability-related needs, services, goals.
It is the most sensitive artifact we have ever designed, and the usual
posture is not sufficient.

**Three requirements, all binding:**

1. **A confidentiality page ships with the product**, not as fine print.
   Where completed sheets may be stored, that they do not go home in a
   folder or sit face-up on a desk, that substitutes get the
   accommodations without the diagnosis, and that they come back at the
   end of the year. Frame it as practical handling guidance, and point
   to the district's own policy and FERPA as the authority rather than
   claiming to state the law ourselves.

2. **The AI page cannot work the way it does elsewhere.** Everywhere
   else we say "de-identified data only" and that suffices, because the
   input is scores. Here the artifact *is* identified data, so the
   ordinary instruction is not enough — a teacher following our usual
   wording could reasonably paste a real sheet. So: **no prompt on this
   product takes anything off a completed sheet.** The AI page instead
   helps the teacher turn a *generic* accommodation into classroom
   practice — "a student needs extended time and chunked directions;
   suggest how that looks in a middle school science lab" — with no
   student, no grade-plus-disability combination, nothing traceable.
   State on the page, plainly, that the completed sheet never goes into
   a chatbot.

3. **Say what it is not.** Not an IEP, not a legal document, not a
   substitute for reading the actual IEP, and not a compliance record.
   A summary for daily practice.

**If any of this cannot be done well today, ship the tracker alone and
hold the at-a-glance for Wednesday.** A privacy misstep on a special
education product would cost far more than a week of delay. The score
is extraordinary; it will still be extraordinary in three days.
