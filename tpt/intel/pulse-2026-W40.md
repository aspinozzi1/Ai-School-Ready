### Three legal claims, each read at the source before printing

A free about copyright that is wrong about copyright would be worse than no free
at all, so all three came from copyright.gov directly, not from a summary:

1. **Circular 33, *Works Not Protected by Copyright*** — copyright does not

   that list.
2. **The Copyright Office FAQ** — "Copyright does not protect names, titles,
   slogans, or short phrases," and then the part teachers miss: "In some cases,
   these things may be protected as trademarks." **That is a different law and
   it is the one that actually catches people.**
3. **copyright.gov/fair-use** — courts "evaluate fair use claims on a
   case-by-case basis" and "there is no formula to ensure that a predetermined
   percentage or amount of a work ... may be used without permission," and
   "This does not mean, however, that all nonprofit education and noncommercial
   uses are fair." **There is no ten-percent rule and there never was.**

### The decision that makes this free worth downloading

**There are no prices in it, on purpose**, and it says so on the cover. AI rates

months. **So it teaches where to look and what to multiply**, which does not go
stale — and prints the one warning that matters: *do not trust a price printed in
any PDF, including this one.*

That decision is also what makes it honest. The alternative was a per-student
figure that would have been wrong by Christmas and quoted in a budget meeting.

### What it actually teaches

**Two different questions wearing one word**, which is where every useless answer
comes from: a **seat** (a person's monthly chatbot subscription — predictable,
scales by headcount) against **usage** (what software pays to call AI by itself —
billed by text, unpredictable until measured). Different bills, different risks,
different permission questions.

**Five mechanics that do not go out of date**, checked against the current API
reference rather than recalled: text is counted in tokens; **input and output are
priced separately and generated text costs several times more**, so a long answer
costs far more than a long document, which is the opposite of what people assume;
**a conversation re-sends its whole history every turn**, the biggest surprise on
a first bill; repeated context can be cached for roughly a tenth of the input
rate; and work that can wait runs at about half price in batch mode, which almost
nobody uses.

Then the method, which is the seam's own sentence: **measure one real run and
multiply.** Including the multiplier people forget — not students, but students
**times how many times each**, which is a factor of thirty between a tool used
once a term and one used every lesson.

**And the cost nobody puts in the spreadsheet: teacher time.** An hour checking
what AI produced is larger than the subscription, and leaving it out makes the
comparison dishonest.

### The research check changed the product, and that is the story

**I set out to write "research shows labeling harms students." It does not say
that.** Kulik and Kulik's 1984 meta-analysis of 31 studies — read at the ERIC
record, not from a summary — reports ability grouping having **"significant
positive effects on the academic performance of elementary school children"**,
a small benefit, and on the exact point this free is about, **"trivially small
effects on the self-concepts of elementary school pupils."**

That is close to the opposite of the page I was about to write. The concerns
about students in low groups internalizing what the group says about them are
real but contested, and later work is mixed both ways.

**So the argument was rebuilt, and it is sharper for it: the case for hiding the
tier is a design case, not a research case.** The label does no teaching work —
ask what a student is supposed to do differently on learning they are on tier
two and there is no answer. **It is a fact about them rather than about their
work.** That turns it from a brave decision into a cheap one: you are removing
something with no upside and an argued-about downside.

**The free says all of this out loud**, names the meta-analysis, and quotes the
"trivially small" finding against itself. A product that tells a teacher the
evidence is weaker than they assumed is a product they trust on the next page.
