## (a) THE BUILD — Test It Like a User

**Lane B build 3 in the spec:** usability testing, feedback rounds and iteration,
the part schools never teach.

### Title, ≤80 characters

**`Vibe Coding for Kids | Test It Like a User | The Day Your Class Breaks Your App`** (79)

Alternative if the Priority 1 rows came back and `design thinking` is the
reachable one: lead on that instead and keep this as the subtitle.

### The product problem, and the decision taken

Wednesday's worksheet raised it and recommended a fix. **No owner answer came,
so the brief decides and says so. An owner instruction overrules this.**

**The problem:** builds 1 and 2 each shipped their own app. **Build 3 tests an
app the buyer may not have.** A unit whose first instruction is "use the app you
built in a different product" is one a buyer feels cheated by.

**The decision: ship an app to test, and ship it deliberately flawed.**

Not badly made. **Made with real, findable usability problems**, of the kind every
first build has. Students cannot find usability problems in a polished app, and a
class that finds nothing concludes testing is a formality. The teacher's guide
lists what is wrong with it, so the teacher knows what their class should be
finding and can tell whether the testing is working.

**It is also the honest version of the lesson:** you cannot see these problems
from the inside. That is the entire reason user testing exists, and a flawed app
teaches it in a way a perfect one cannot.

## (b) THE APP — The Makerspace Booker

**A slot booker for a shared room or a piece of equipment.** Pick a day, pick a
slot, book it; slots show as free or taken.

### Why this app and not another cart

Build 2 was a store with a cart. **Repeating that shape would make build 3 feel
like the same unit again.** A booking grid is structurally different, universally
understandable without any subject knowledge, and rich in exactly the kind of
usability problems that are findable by an eleven-year-old.

**And it needs no names at all**, which solves the data question before it is
asked — see section (e).

### The six flaws it ships with, and they are the product

Every one is a classic, is findable by a student in a two-minute task, and is
fixable by prompting on day 5. **The app must not crash and must not lose data** —
these are usability problems, not bugs.

| # | The flaw | What a tester will say |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The main button says **"Submit"** but it only saves a draft; a second, smaller button actually books | "I thought I'd booked it" |
| 2 | **No confirmation** after booking. The screen looks identical | "Did that work?" |
| 3 | The error says only **"Error"** when a slot is already taken | "It won't let me and it won't say why" |
| 4 | **The steps only work in one order** — pick the day before the slot — and nothing says so | "It keeps clearing what I picked" |
| 5 | **Cancel is tiny and buried**; "Book the whole day" is the biggest button on screen | "I nearly booked the whole day by accident" |
| 6 | The confirmation code is shown once and **needed later on a different screen** | "I didn't know I had to write that down" |

**The teacher's guide lists all six with the sentence a tester is likely to say**,
so the teacher can tell whether their class is finding real things or guessing.

**Do not fix them in the shipped file.** The fixed version is what the class
produces on day 5.

### What the app must do, so the factory can test it

- One HTML file, opens by double-click, offline, no accounts.
- A week of days, a handful of slots each, free or taken.
- Book, cancel, and see the grid update. **Bookings survive a reload.**
- **No name field anywhere** and no record of who booked.
- Save the booker as the teacher's own file, so a fixed version can be kept.
- All six flaws present and reproducible.
- **Zero console errors**, because a crash is not a usability finding.

## (c) THE FIVE-DAY ARC

| Day | Students do | Teacher does |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Watch somebody struggle** | Give one person a task and watch without helping. Write what they *did*, not what they said. | Model it badly first — helping, explaining, leading — then well. |
| **2 · Run real tests** | Three testers each, same three tasks, silent observation. Findings on paper. | Enforce the one rule: **if you have to explain it, that is a finding.** |
| **3 · Sort and argue** | Group findings. Decide which are real and which are one person's preference. | Push on "everybody said" when two people said it. |
| **4 · Pick three, and justify** | Choose three fixes from all the findings and write why each one, with the evidence. | Refuse any fix with no observation behind it. |
| **5 · Fix one, live, and re-test** | Watch the fix happen, then **re-test it on a new person.** | Prompt on the board. Then make them test it again. |

**Day 5 is what makes this a unit rather than an activity.** Almost nobody
re-tests. A class that fixes something and then watches it still confuse somebody
has learned the actual lesson, which is that a fix is a hypothesis.

### The three tasks to give testers, written out

Put these in the pack word for word, because a vague task produces a vague test:

1. *"Book the makerspace for Thursday, first slot after lunch."*
2. *"You booked Tuesday by mistake. Cancel it."*
3. *"Find the earliest free slot tomorrow and take it."*

Each one walks a tester straight into at least two of the six flaws.

## (e) THE DATA RULE, AND THIS ONE IS EASY FOR A REASON

The tier is decided by **where data lives and who can reach it**, not by whether
names appear. **This build was designed so the hard case never arises.**

- **Tier 1 — build freely.** The booking grid, the slots, the room's name, the
  three tasks, the findings sheets. None of it is about a child.
- **Tier 2 — the teacher's own device.** The saved booker file and the fixed
  version the class produces. Transmitted nowhere.
- **Tier 3 — stop.** Any of it attached to a named student.

**The app has no name field, so there is nothing to tier.** Say that in the pack
and say why it was built that way: a booking system that records who booked what,
when, is a movement record about identified children — the same objection as the
hall pass build, which this store declined to make.

**And the testing rule, which is the one that matters here:** findings sheets
record **what a tester did**, never who they were. "Tester 2 clicked Submit and
stopped" is a finding. Naming the child who struggled in front of the class is
how this unit goes wrong, and the pack says so on the student page.

## (g) THE FAILURE MODES SPECIFIC TO THIS BUILD

1. **Students help the tester.** The single hardest rule in the unit and the one
   that breaks it. Day 1 exists to make the helping instinct visible before it
   ruins the data.
2. **"It was fine" findings.** Not a finding. The pack needs the forcing
   question: *what did they do, exactly, at the moment they paused?*
3. **A class that finds nothing**, which means the flaws were too subtle. The
   teacher's guide lists all six, so the teacher can steer a task toward one.
4. **A class that finds everything and fixes nothing.** Day 4 caps it at three
   with evidence required, which is the real-world constraint.
5. **The fix makes it worse**, discovered in the day 5 re-test. **This is a
   success, not a failure**, and the pack should say so plainly.
6. **Testers are classmates who already know the app.** Day 2 should use people
   who have not seen it — another class, a sibling, an adult in the building.
