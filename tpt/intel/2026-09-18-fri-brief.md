## (b) The build — Pitch to Prototype

A unit where a class finds a real problem in their own school, specs an app to
fix it, and watches the teacher build it live. The deliverable is working
software, not a poster.

**The design law, and it is not a compromise:** the grown-up holds the
keyboard. Chatbots are 13+, and no child's identity enters a prompt. Students
do the market research, the problem statement, the feature list, the user
testing, the pitch and the iteration calls. The teacher does the prompting and
the building, projected so the class watches their own spec become real.

**That division is the better lesson.** Specification, testing and judgment
are the actual thinking. Typing prompts is not.

### The five-day arc

| Day | Students do | Teacher does |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Find the problem** | Interview three people in the building about something annoying. Not "an app idea" — a problem. | Model one interview badly, then well. |
| **2 · Define it** | Turn interviews into one problem statement and a must-have / nice-to-have feature list. | Push back on features. "Must-have" should be three things, not eleven. |
| **3 · Build it live** | Read the spec aloud; watch it become software; call out what is wrong. | Prompt on the board. Let them see it fail and get fixed. |
| **4 · Test it** | Usability testing on real users from day 1. Watch, do not explain. | Teach the rule: if you have to explain it, that is a finding. |
| **5 · Pitch and iterate** | Pitch the app with evidence from testing. Decide what to change and what to leave. | Make one live change from their decision. Ship it. |

### What is in the pack (~26 pages)

Teacher guide and how to run each day · **student pages worth printing**:
interview sheet, problem statement frame, must-have/nice-to-have sorter, test
observation sheet, pitch planner, iteration decision log · **the exact teacher
prompts written out in full** · how to run a live build without it derailing ·
**what to do when the AI produces something broken in front of thirty
children** (a page, not a sentence — it will happen, and how the teacher
reacts is the lesson) · an example finished app so a teacher can see the
target · how this is built, and what it is not.

### The page nobody else will write

**"It broke in front of the class."** The AI will produce something that does
not work, mid-lesson, with thirty children watching. That is not a failure of
the lesson — it is the most valuable ten minutes in it, if the teacher knows
what to do. The page gives them the words: name what went wrong out loud, ask
the class what they saw, fix it in front of them. **Modelling how an adult
handles something broken is worth more than the app.**

  about situations, not people.
